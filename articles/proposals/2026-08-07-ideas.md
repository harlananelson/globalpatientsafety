Five data-driven article proposals grounded in pharmacovigilance developments from the
last ~1–2 months, matched to what this site already does: independent disproportionality
reanalysis of FAERS/VAERS using the four-method engine (GPS/EBGM, PRR, ROR, BCPNN/IC),
EB05-thresholded signals with ≥2 methods agreeing, latest-quarter snapshot **plus**
quarterly caterpillar trajectory, observed counts shown beside every EB05.

**Editorial lane (per `ARTICLE_QUEUE.md`):** methodology first. Harlan is a statistician,
not an MD, so each piece is framed as a transferable "reading FDA adverse-event data" lesson
anchored to a concrete drug/vaccine example — *not* a clinical causation claim. Voice
reference: the Christine Cotton VAERS piece — modest, testable claims; explicit about what
disproportionality can and cannot show; careful with stimulated-reporting, litigation-cluster,

## 1. A boxed warning was *removed* — does the FAERS record agree the danger was never there?

**Working title:** "When the Warning Goes Away: Testosterone, TRAVERSE, and Using
Disproportionality as a *Negative* Check"

**Safety question.** Almost every piece this site has written asks whether a signal is *real*.
This one flips it: after the TRAVERSE randomized trial found no excess of major adverse cardiac
events, the FDA **removed** the cardiovascular boxed warning from the entire testosterone class —
and in the same action *added* a new increased-blood-pressure warning. So two testable questions
follow. (a) Does independent FAERS disproportionality for testosterone against the cardiovascular
event cluster (MI, stroke, PE, DVT) show *no* signal, corroborating the de-escalation — i.e., can
disproportionality serve as a **negative control** that agrees with an RCT? (b) Does the *blood-pressure*
signal that replaced it actually show up in spontaneous reports? A published FAERS study already
reports RORs **below 1** (no disproportionality) for PE, DVT, and MI with testosterone cypionate —
a rare, clean example of "the database says nothing, and nothing is the correct answer."

**Why it matters now.** The de-escalation is live and still moving: in **June 2026** HHS, through
the FDA, formally requested further testosterone-label updates (removing the age-related-hypogonadism
limitation-of-use and revising prostate-cancer-risk language), extending the class-wide relabeling
that in **February 2025** removed the cardiovascular boxed warning and required a new blood-pressure
warning, both driven by the **TRAVERSE** trial (5,204 men; MACE 7.0% testosterone vs 7.3% placebo
over ~33 months). And according to PubMed, a **January 2026** FAERS study (Kandukuru et al.,
*Curr Drug Saf*) found RORs of 0.039 (PE), 0.133 (DVT), and 0.83 (MI) — all below the
disproportionality threshold — for testosterone cypionate. A regulator walking a warning *back*,
plus an RCT, plus a spontaneous-reporting null, is an unusually complete natural experiment.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Run GPS/EBGM + PRR + ROR + BCPNN for testosterone
(pooling salts/formulations, then split cypionate vs topical AndroGel as a within-class comparator)
against two PT clusters: the *retired-warning* cardiovascular cluster (*Myocardial infarction*,
*Cerebrovascular accident*, *Pulmonary embolism*, *Deep vein thrombosis*, *Cardiac arrest*) and the
*new-warning* cluster (*Blood pressure increased*, *Hypertension*). Show observed counts beside every
EB05 and the quarterly caterpillar across the Feb-2025 relabel date. The **methodology centerpiece** is
threefold: (1) disproportionality as a *negative* check — a null EB05 can corroborate a warning
*removal*, which is a use of the method the site hasn't demonstrated; (2) the ceiling of spontaneous
reporting for a *causal* cardiovascular question — an RCT (TRAVERSE) outranks any amount of FAERS
signal, so a flat EB05 is consistent with, but cannot by itself prove, safety; (3) whether the new
blood-pressure signal is even *reportable* (BP elevation is under-reported to FAERS relative to
hard events). Explicitly separates "the database is silent because the risk is absent" from "…because
the event is unreportable."

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low.** Testosterone is high-volume with deep FAERS coverage and
standard cardiovascular PTs; the cypionate-vs-AndroGel split and the relabel-date trajectory are the
only real wrangling, and both are straightforward. The value is conceptual (the negative-check framing),
a strong fit for the methodology-first lane.

## 2. The signal that got louder *after* the warning — reading stimulated reporting as a dated natural experiment

**Working title:** "Did Fezolinetant Get More Dangerous, or Did People Start Looking? A Boxed
Warning as a Reporting-Rate Natural Experiment"

**Safety question.** Fezolinetant (Veozah), the first non-hormonal NK3-receptor antagonist for
menopausal hot flashes, received an FDA **boxed warning for rare serious liver injury** in
December 2024. A just-published FAERS study finds strong hepatic signals — AST/ALT elevations with
RORs near 40–48 — *and* explicitly reports that liver-related signals were **stronger in reports
submitted after 2024**, which the authors attribute to *stimulated reporting following the FDA safety
communication*. That is the site's "signals are non-stationary / stimulated-reporting" gotcha caught in
the act, with a known communication date to anchor it. The question: can our caterpillar show the
hepatic-signal *reporting rate* stepping up right at the boxed-warning date — and can we separate the
attention-driven jump from any genuine change in the underlying liver risk?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Wei S, Song Q, Wang L, "Real-world safety profile of
fezolinetant: a disproportionality analysis based on the FAERS database," *Naunyn Schmiedebergs Arch
Pharmacol*, published **August 4, 2026** (FAERS 2023Q2–2026Q1), reported 20 PT-level signals meeting
all four algorithms — dominated by liver-enzyme abnormalities (ALT increased ROR 48.31; AST increased
ROR 41.72), a median onset of 38 days (within the FDA-flagged early-injury window), *a novel "nightmare"
signal*, and — critically — **stronger, more numerous liver signals in post-2024 reports, "which may
partly reflect stimulated reporting following FDA safety communications"**
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05773-0)). The regulatory anchor is concrete and dated:
FDA added the boxed warning on **December 16, 2024**. Menopause therapy is high-interest, and a piece
that teaches "a rising signal can be rising *attention*, not rising *risk*" extends the site's gotcha
catalogue with the cleanest possible worked example: a fixed, public date to test against.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Four-method engine for fezolinetant against the
hepatic PT cluster (*Alanine aminotransferase increased*, *Aspartate aminotransferase increased*,
*Gamma-glutamyltransferase increased*, *Blood bilirubin increased*, *Drug-induced liver injury*,
*Hepatocellular injury*), showing observed counts beside every EB05. The **methodological payload** is
the quarterly caterpillar bisected by the **Dec-2024 boxed-warning date**: if the EB05 *and the report
volume* both step up at that date, the increment is a stimulated-reporting artifact (attention), not new
biology — the same natural-experiment logic as the NAION/GLP-1 emergence-timing check, but applied to a
*single* drug's own warning. Pair with a time-to-onset view (median ~38 days) to show the early-injury
window is real even though the magnitude is attention-inflated, and treat the "nightmare" PT as a
teachable *small-N, single-source* candidate (it attenuated in the physician-only sensitivity analysis —
a built-in lesson on report-source robustness).

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** Fezolinetant is a 2023 launch (moderate volume), so the
hepatic PTs are well-populated but the drug's total report count is modest; the bisect-at-the-warning-date
trajectory and a report-source (physician vs consumer) split are the analytically interesting steps, both
tractable in the existing pipeline.

  disproportionality analysis based on the FAERS database," *Naunyn Schmiedebergs Arch Pharmacol* 2026 —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05773-0)
- FDA Drug Safety Communication, "FDA adds warning about rare occurrence of serious liver injury with use

## 3. *When* an adverse event shows up is a signal too — time-to-onset and a cross-database corroboration

**Working title:** "Early-Failure, Not Random: What the *Timing* of Reports Adds to Disproportionality
(Glatiramer Anaphylaxis in FAERS and Canada)"

**Safety question.** Every article the site has published reads the *magnitude* of a signal (EB05, ROR).
But two other dimensions are usually left on the table: **when** the event tends to occur after starting
the drug (time-to-onset, and its Weibull shape) and whether an **independent national database** shows the
same pattern. Glatiramer acetate (Copaxone/Glatopa) carries an FDA **boxed warning for anaphylaxis** added
in January 2025; a just-published study characterizes that signal across *both* the US FAERS and the
Canadian CVAR databases and finds an "**early-failure**" Weibull pattern (events cluster early after
initiation, not at random). Can the site demonstrate these two under-used tools — a time-to-onset curve and
a second-database replication — as a way to *strengthen* (or puncture) a disproportionality signal beyond
its point estimate?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Nie T, Liu H, Cheng C, et al., "Reported Characteristics of
Anaphylaxis Associated With Glatiramer Acetate: A Pharmacovigilance Analysis of the FDA and Canadian
Databases," *Pharmacol Res Perspect*, published **August 2026** (FAERS Q1 2004–Q1 2025; CVAR Q1 1996–Q1
2025), identified 9,816 FAERS reports with 29 significant disproportionality signals, an **early-failure**
time-to-onset pattern across all signals, and 17 overlapping preferred terms between the two databases
(with *respiratory arrest* appearing uniquely in the Canadian data) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/prp2.70303)).
The regulatory anchor is dated and concrete: the FDA added the anaphylaxis **boxed warning on January 22,
2025**, after reviewing 82 global cases (6 deaths). A methods piece that introduces *time-to-onset* and
*cross-database corroboration* — neither of which the site has shown yet — is a natural next chapter in
the "Reading FDA Adverse-Event Data" series.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS** (plus, if reachable, a public Canada Vigilance extract as
the confirmatory set). Four-method engine for glatiramer against the anaphylaxis/hypersensitivity PT
cluster (*Anaphylactic reaction*, *Anaphylactic shock*, *Hypersensitivity*, *Angioedema*, *Dyspnoea*),
observed counts beside every EB05. Then the two **new methodological tools** as the article's spine:
(1) a **time-to-onset distribution** with a Weibull shape parameter — an "early-failure" shape (events
front-loaded after initiation) is mechanistically coherent for an immediate hypersensitivity reaction and
adds evidence a bare ROR cannot; (2) **cross-database corroboration** — does an independent national system
flag the same PTs? Agreement raises confidence a signal isn't a US-reporting artifact; a PT that appears in
only one system (e.g., *respiratory arrest*) is a lesson in database-specific coding/capture. Frame both as
transferable techniques, with the standard caveat that time-to-onset is only as good as the (often missing)
onset dates in the reports.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** The FAERS disproportionality is routine, but time-to-onset
requires parsing therapy-start and event dates (frequently missing/als noisy — itself worth stating), and
the cross-database step depends on obtaining a comparable Canada Vigilance extract. If CVAR access is
impractical, the piece still stands on the FAERS time-to-onset tool alone, with the published paper's
cross-database result cited as external corroboration.

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Nie T, Liu H, Cheng C, Li J, Lu J, "Reported Characteristics of Anaphylaxis
  Associated With Glatiramer Acetate: A Pharmacovigilance Analysis of the FDA and Canadian Databases,"
  *Pharmacol Res Perspect* 2026;14(4):e70303 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/prp2.70303)
- FDA Drug Safety Communication, "FDA adds Boxed Warning about a rare but serious allergic reaction called
  anaphylaxis with the multiple sclerosis medicine glatiramer acetate (Copaxone, Glatopa)" (Jan 22, 2025) —
  https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-adds-boxed-warning-about-rare-serious-allergic-reaction-called-anaphylaxis-multiple-sclerosis

## 4. The class carries the warning — but do the newest, least-used members actually earn it?

**Working title:** "Not All Fluoroquinolones Are the Same: A Boxed-Warning Class Effect vs a Small-N
Newcomer Signal"

**Safety question.** Fluoroquinolones (FQs) carry one of the FDA's most famous class actions — a 2016
boxed-warning restriction plus long-standing peripheral-neuropathy (PN) warnings — issued for *the class*.
But regulators warn classes; patients take *specific agents*. Two fresh 2026 FAERS studies find the PN
signal is **not uniform**: it is strongest for the high-volume agents (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin,
moxifloxacin, ofloxacin), and — more interesting for methodology — surfaces even for **gemifloxacin**, a
low-volume newer FQ with little prior neurotoxicity evaluation. When a class warning meets a rarely-used
member, is a positive EB05 a genuine shared class effect or a small-N mirage? Can the site show, agent by
agent, where the class warning is well-earned and where the evidence is too thin to say?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, two 2026 analyses put this on the table. Shamim N et al.,
"Disproportionality Analysis of Fluoroquinolone-Associated Peripheral Neuropathy in the FAERS Database
(2007–2024)," *Clin Transl Sci*, **April 2026**, found positive PN signals across the four common FQs *and*
detected PN signals for **gemifloxacin** (a newer agent with limited prior evaluation), with combination
FQ therapy producing signals exceeding monotherapy ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/cts.70541)); and Rusu A
et al., "An Updated 16-Year Pharmacovigilance Analysis of Neuropsychiatric Safety Profiles of Ciprofloxacin,
Levofloxacin, and Moxifloxacin Using FAERS Data," *Pharmaceuticals (Basel)*, **May 2026** (95,968 reports),
found PN was the strongest *neurological* signal for levofloxacin and moxifloxacin while ciprofloxacin
skewed toward CNS effects — a "**non-interchangeable safety profile**" within the class
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ph19060820)). FQs are prescribed to millions, and a "the warning is on the
class, but read the agent" piece is broadly useful and squarely methodological.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Four-method engine for **each** of the six FDA-approved
FQs (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, ofloxacin, gemifloxacin, delafloxacin) against a PN PT
cluster (*Peripheral neuropathy*, *Paraesthesia*, *Hypoaesthesia*, *Peripheral sensory neuropathy*,
*Burning sensation*, *Small fibre neuropathy*), with **observed counts front-and-center** because the
whole lesson lives in the denominator. Two methodological payloads: (1) the **class-vs-member** contrast —
which agents carry a stable, well-populated EB05 and which rest on a handful of reports (gemifloxacin,
delafloxacin), where a positive point estimate with a wide interval should *not* be read as a discovery;
and (2) the **combination-therapy amplification** the Shamim paper flags — when reports name two FQs,
attributing the event to a specific agent is impossible (a confounding/attribution caveat). Ties directly
to the site's "small-N EB05 needs the trajectory before it means anything" and "the raw leaderboard lies"
gotchas.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** The high-volume FQs have deep coverage and trivial PTs; the
work — and the point — is the *low*-volume members, where the honest finding may be "too few reports to
say," which is itself the lesson. Freshness caveat: the anchor papers are April–May 2026 (~3–4 months),
slightly older than the ideal 1–2-month window, but new to the site and paired with an unambiguous, dated
class-warning history.

  *Clin Transl Sci* 2026;19(4):e70541 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/cts.70541)
- According to PubMed: Rusu A, Stroia IM, Cherecheș MC, "An Updated 16-Year Pharmacovigilance Analysis of

## 5. A drug approved two weeks ago has no signal yet — so what does a *baseline* even look like?

**Working title:** "Before the First Signal: Setting the FAERS Baseline for Centanafadine (Simtriyo),
a Brand-New ADHD Drug"

**Safety question.** Disproportionality needs reports, and a drug approved on July 24, 2026 has almost
none. So the honest first move for any new approval isn't a signal hunt — it's a **baseline**: what would
have to accumulate before an EB05 means anything, which adverse events the *mechanism* tells us to watch,
and how to avoid over-reading the first trickle of reports (which are dominated by trial-known, expected
events and reporter enthusiasm). Centanafadine (Simtriyo), the first norepinephrine-dopamine-serotonin
triple-reuptake inhibitor (NDSRI) for ADHD, is an ideal worked example: a novel mechanism in a drug class
(stimulant-like ADHD agents) with well-known watch-items (cardiovascular/blood-pressure effects, appetite
suppression, psychiatric events), so the *a-priori* watch-list is clear before any FAERS data exist.

**Why it matters now.** The FDA approved **Simtriyo (centanafadine)** on **July 24, 2026** for ADHD in
adults and children ≥6 (Otsuka), the first approved NDSRI — a genuinely new mechanism of action. That
recency *is* the point: this is the rare window in which the site can publish the *pre-signal* discipline —
define the watch-list and the "how many reports until EB05 is interpretable" thresholds **before** the
inevitable first anecdotes arrive, then check back in a future piece. The genre matches earlier proposals
(teplizumab pediatric baseline, infant-RSV VAERS baseline, oral-GLP-1 fingerprint), but the drug and its
mechanism are new, and the timing is maximally fresh.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Because centanafadine will have near-zero reports, the
article is *methodological by necessity*: (1) show the empty/near-empty FAERS query and explain *why*
disproportionality is undefined at low counts (the EB05 shrinkage and PRR instability that make early
estimates meaningless); (2) build the **mechanism-driven watch-list** — a cardiovascular/BP cluster
(*Blood pressure increased*, *Heart rate increased*, *Palpitations*), an appetite/GI cluster (*Decreased
appetite*, *Nausea*), and a psychiatric cluster (*Insomnia*, *Anxiety*, *Irritability*) — grounded in the
approved-label adverse reactions and the NDSRI pharmacology; (3) establish an **active-comparator frame**
in advance (centanafadine vs an established ADHD agent such as atomoxetine or lisdexamfetamine) so that
when reports do accrue, the comparison is against a like drug, not the whole database (the confounding-by-
indication trap). The transferable lesson: the first, most disciplined pharmacovigilance act for a new
drug is deciding *what would count as a signal* before any data can bias the question.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low (by design).** There is essentially no data yet — which is the entire
premise. The work is conceptual: the label-based watch-list, the count-threshold explainer, and the
pre-registered comparator choice. Freshness caveat: precisely because the drug is two weeks old, this piece
is a *forward-looking* baseline, not a findings piece — best framed explicitly as "part 1, revisit in N
quarters."
