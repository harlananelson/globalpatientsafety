# Article Ideas — Week of 2026-09-11

Five data-driven article proposals grounded in pharmacovigilance developments from the
last ~1–2 months, matched to what this site already does: independent disproportionality
reanalysis of FAERS/VAERS using the four-method engine (GPS/EBGM, PRR, ROR, BCPNN/IC),
EB05-thresholded signals with ≥2 methods agreeing, latest-quarter snapshot **plus**
quarterly caterpillar trajectory, observed counts shown beside every EB05.

**Editorial lane (per `ARTICLE_QUEUE.md`):** methodology first. Harlan is a statistician,
not an MD, so each piece is framed as a transferable "reading FDA adverse-event data" lesson
anchored to a concrete drug/vaccine example — *not* a clinical causation claim. Voice
reference: the Christine Cotton VAERS piece — modest, testable claims; explicit about what
disproportionality can and cannot show; careful with stimulated-reporting, litigation-cluster,
confounding-by-indication, and coding-artifact caveats.

**These are proposals only.** No analyses have been run and no numbers below are computed
from our data — every quantitative statement is sourced to a regulator or the peer-reviewed
literature, not to our parquet. Numbers from published papers are *their* numbers; the point of
each article is to reproduce (or fail to reproduce) them in our own four-method engine.

**Deduplication check.** These avoid the current queue (Zostavax-vs-Shingrix zoster, GLP-1→NAION

## 1. A brand-new boxed warning that should be a *clean* disproportionality signal — and a built-in negative control

**Working title:** "When Disproportionality Gets It Right: Reading the New Ferric-Carboxymaltose
Hypophosphatemia Boxed Warning — With Ferric Derisomaltose as the Control"

**Safety question.** In August 2026 the FDA added a **boxed warning for hypophosphatemia** to
ferric carboxymaltose (Injectafer) after identifying serious, symptomatic cases in its
adverse-event data. Unlike most warnings the site has examined (built on tiny case counts, or
dominated by confounding), this one sits on top of an *unusually clean* pharmacology: FCM causes
hypophosphatemia far more often than other IV irons, and randomized head-to-head data quantify
the gap. So this is the rare setting where the site can ask the *positive* version of its usual
question: does the four-method engine reproduce a real, mechanistically-understood class
difference — a strong FCM → hypophosphatemia signal that is **absent or much weaker** for ferric
derisomaltose and iron sucrose? And does the clinically important *downstream* harm the warning is
really about (hypophosphatemic osteomalacia → fractures) show up at all, or is only the lab
abnormality reported?

**Why it matters now.** On **August 13, 2026** the FDA granted a citizen petition seeking stronger
safety labeling for ferric carboxymaltose, and a **boxed warning for hypophosphatemia** was added
to the Injectafer label (labeling update ~September 2026). FDA's action cited **134 reports** of
serious, symptomatic hypophosphatemia associated with ferric carboxymaltose over **July 1, 2022 –
June 30, 2025**. The differential class effect is well established by randomized trials: the
PHOSPHARE-IBD trial and its supporting literature review reported pooled hypophosphatemia of
**~47% after FCM vs ~4% after ferric derisomaltose**. This is a live, this-quarter FDA labeling
action with a spontaneous-reporting spine — exactly what the site is built to examine.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Run GPS/EBGM + PRR + ROR + BCPNN for **ferric
carboxymaltose** against a phosphate cluster — *Hypophosphataemia*, *Blood phosphorus decreased*,
*Osteomalacia*, *Bone pain*, and the downstream *Fracture*/*Pathological fracture* PTs — and run
the **same** analysis for **ferric derisomaltose (Monoferric)**, **iron sucrose (Venofer)**,
**ferumoxytol**, and **iron dextran** as within-class comparators. The teaching payload is a
positive-control / negative-control demonstration: FCM should flag hard (4/4) while ferric
derisomaltose should not — a case where disproportionality *correctly* separates a class, which is
worth showing precisely because the site so often shows the opposite. Second lesson: contrast the
well-populated lab-abnormality PT (*Hypophosphataemia*) against the sparser but clinically decisive
*Osteomalacia*/*Fracture* PTs, and use the quarterly caterpillar to check whether reporting jumps
after the 2026 warning (a natural-experiment date, as in the NAION piece). State plainly the
channelling caveat (FCM is the highest-volume IV iron, so raw counts favor it) — which is exactly
why the four-method *disproportionality* comparison, not raw counts, is the honest lens.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** All IV irons are well-represented in FAERS with
standard PTs; the phosphate and fracture PTs are unambiguous. The only real work is a clean
mapping of the several IV-iron ingredient strings and honest handling of the volume difference —
which is itself the article's methods content.

## 2. The signal that only appears when you split the table by sex

**Working title:** "Beyond the Pooled Table: Why an Antiarrhythmic's Torsades Signal Can Hide Until
You Stratify FAERS by Sex"

**Safety question.** Drug-induced QT prolongation and torsades de pointes are among the best-known
*sex-differentiated* drug harms — women carry roughly double the risk, driven by a longer baseline
QTc and reduced repolarization reserve, not by drug levels. A whole-database disproportionality run
pools men and women, averaging over the exact axis on which the risk splits. A brand-new FAERS
study (Sept 2026) argues explicitly for going "beyond pooled analysis" and finds sex-specific
cardiovascular reporting signals for antiarrhythmic drugs. Can the site show, with its own engine,
that a torsades/QT signal for a QT-prolonging antiarrhythmic (sotalol, dofetilide, quinidine,
ibutilide) can be *diluted in the pooled table but sharp once stratified by sex* — a clean,
transferable lesson on aggregation bias?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Khalifa A, et al., "Beyond pooled analysis:
sex-specific cardiovascular adverse event reporting signals with antiarrhythmic drugs in FAERS,"
*Naunyn-Schmiedeberg's Archives of Pharmacology*, published **September 9, 2026**
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05837-1)), makes sex-stratified disproportionality its
central method. It lands on a decades-old, well-documented pharmacology — the ~2-fold female excess
in drug-induced long-QT/torsades — that most routine FAERS analyses (including several the site has
critiqued) silently average away. That pairing of a *fresh methods paper* with a *textbook
sex-difference* makes this the ideal vehicle for a stratification lesson the site's gotcha
catalogue does not yet contain.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** FAERS carries a patient-sex field, so the
four-method engine can be run (a) pooled and (b) within male and female strata for an antiarrhythmic
against a proarrhythmia PT cluster — *Torsade de pointes*, *Electrocardiogram QT prolonged*,
*Ventricular tachycardia*, *Ventricular fibrillation*, *Cardiac arrest*, *Sudden death*. Show the
pooled EB05 beside the two sex-specific EB05s with observed counts, and the quarterly caterpillar
for each stratum. The methodology payload: (1) pooling can mask or dilute a signal that is
concentrated in one sex (an aggregation/Simpson-flavored trap); (2) the *fix* — always ask whether
a known effect-modifier (here sex) is being averaged over — is transferable to age, indication, and
formulation. Caveats stated plainly: FAERS sex-field completeness, differential reporting by sex,
and that a *reporting* difference is not an *incidence* difference (the honest limit that makes the
known clinical sex-difference a useful external anchor rather than a claim the data can prove).

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** Antiarrhythmics and the torsades/QT PTs are well-covered,
and the sex field is standard. The real work is disciplined handling of missing-sex reports and
resisting over-interpretation of small within-stratum counts — which is the article's point.

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Khalifa A, Dadam M, Aldib A, Salem Othman, "Beyond pooled analysis:
  sex-specific cardiovascular adverse event reporting signals with antiarrhythmic drugs in FAERS,"
  *Naunyn Schmiedebergs Arch Pharmacol* 2026 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05837-1)
- Makkar RR, et al. (classic), "Female gender as a risk factor for torsades de pointes associated
  with cardiovascular drugs" / "Drug-induced long QT syndrome in women: review of current evidence
  and remaining gaps" (~2-fold female risk; reduced repolarization reserve) —
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18573480/
- "Sex-Related Differences in Drug-Induced QT Prolongation and Torsades de Pointes: A New Model

  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30247688/

## 3. When a real cardiac signal is *mediated* by a known reaction, not the drug's direct toxicity

**Working title:** "Is It the Drug or the Cytokine Storm? Reading Bispecific-Antibody Cardiac
Signals Without Confusing Mechanism"

**Safety question.** The bispecific T-cell engagers now transforming myeloma and lymphoma therapy —
teclistamab (Tecvayli), elranatamab (Elrexfio), talquetamab (Talvey), epcoritamab (Epkinly),
mosunetuzumab (Lunsumio), glofitamab (Columvi) — all carry warnings for cytokine release syndrome
(CRS). A July 2026 FAERS pharmacovigilance analysis reports striking cardiac signals, including a
**myocarditis reporting-odds ratio of ~25.7 for teclistamab** and a hypotension signal for
epcoritamab. But hypotension, tachycardia, and even myocarditis-like presentations are also
*downstream of CRS itself*. So the honest methods question is one disproportionality can't answer
alone but must be *framed*: is the cardiac signal a direct cardiotoxic effect, or the fingerprint of
a known on-target reaction (CRS) that these drugs are expected to cause? A signal can be entirely
real and still not mean "the drug is directly cardiotoxic."

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Munir A, Sayed A, Lee S, Ghazi S, Houmsse M, Badat N,
Moeed A, Guha A, Addison D, Epperla N, "Cardiovascular Toxicity Associated With Bispecific
Antibodies in Hematological Malignancies: A Comprehensive Pharmacovigilance Analysis," *American
Journal of Hematology* 2026;101(10):2546–2556, published **July 24, 2026**
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajh.70449)), reports the teclistamab myocarditis and epcoritamab
hypotension signals above, and notes prior-anthracycline exposure as an independent mortality
correlate. Corroborating FAERS work on bispecifics in B-cell lymphoma and on monoclonal antibodies
in myeloma appeared the same season. This is a fast-growing, recently-approved drug class where the
"reading the signal correctly" question is genuinely unsettled — a strong methodology-lane fit.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Four-method engine for each bispecific against a
cardiac PT cluster — *Myocarditis*, *Cardiac failure*, *Hypotension*, *Tachycardia*, *Atrial
fibrillation*, *Cardiac arrest* — with observed counts and the quarterly caterpillar. The
methodology payload is the *attribution* lesson: (1) run the cardiac PTs beside *Cytokine release
syndrome* for the same drugs and show how tightly the cardiac reports co-travel with CRS reports —
disproportionality shows association, not mechanism; (2) confounding by indication and prior therapy
(heavily pretreated patients, anthracycline history) inflate cardiac reporting independent of the
bispecific; (3) the transferable rule: before calling a signal "direct organ toxicity," ask whether
it is the expected sequela of a *known* reaction the drug causes. Explicitly frames what FAERS can
(flag a reporting association) and cannot (separate CRS-mediated from direct cardiotoxicity, or give
incidence) settle.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** The class is new (2022–2025 approvals), so per-drug counts
are modest and several PTs will be small-N — which is exactly why the CRS co-reporting framing, not a
headline ROR, is the honest read. Mapping the six ingredient strings and separating CRS from cardiac
PTs cleanly is the main wrangling.

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Munir A, et al., "Cardiovascular Toxicity Associated With Bispecific
  Antibodies in Hematological Malignancies: A Comprehensive Pharmacovigilance Analysis," *Am J
  Hematol* 2026;101(10):2546–2556 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajh.70449)
- "Cardiovascular adverse events associated with bispecific antibodies in relapsed or refractory
  B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas" (corroborating FAERS analysis, 2026) —
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42192442/

  (context / CRS-cardiac overlap) — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12380792/

## 4. The harm that disproportionality structurally cannot code: antidepressants and persistent sexual dysfunction

**Working title:** "No Term, No Signal: Why FAERS Can Rank On-Treatment Sexual Dysfunction Across
Antidepressants but Is Blind to PSSD"

**Safety question.** Post-SSRI sexual dysfunction (PSSD) — sexual dysfunction that *persists after*
an antidepressant is stopped — is recognized by the EMA and several regulators, but in the US it
appears only on the fluoxetine label, and in November 2025 a request to add PSSD as a standalone
MedDRA term was rejected, so reports are folded into generic sexual-dysfunction PTs. A brand-new
FAERS study (Aug 2026) ranks the *differential* strength of male sexual-dysfunction signals across
antidepressants. That sets up a sharp two-sided methods lesson: disproportionality *can* compare
on-treatment sexual-dysfunction reporting across agents, but it is **structurally blind** to the
defining feature of PSSD — persistence after discontinuation — because (a) there is no PT for it and
(b) spontaneous reporting has no denominator, no reliable off-drug timepoint, and a latency the
method cannot represent. What can the four-method engine legitimately say here, and where must it
stop?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Xu, Liu, Yang, Kong, "Differential signal strength of
male sexual dysfunction associated with antidepressants: a disproportionality analysis of FAERS
database," *Sexual Medicine* 2026;14(5):qfag082, published **August 28, 2026**
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/sexmed/qfag082)), is a fresh, on-topic FAERS analysis. The
regulatory context is concrete and current: PSSD is on the EMA product information since 2019 and
the US fluoxetine label, but **remains absent from other US SSRI/SNRI labels**, and the **November
2025 MedDRA rejection** means the persistent form has no dedicated coded term — an ongoing,
this-year data-infrastructure story that directly shapes what any FAERS analysis of this harm can
detect.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) The *measurable* half: run the four-method
engine across SSRIs/SNRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, escitalopram, venlafaxine,
duloxetine) against the coded sexual-dysfunction PTs — *Sexual dysfunction*, *Libido decreased*,
*Erectile dysfunction*, *Anorgasmia*, *Ejaculation disorder* — with observed counts and the
caterpillar, reproducing the paper's differential-ranking design. (2) The *methodology centerpiece*
— the negative-space lesson: PSSD's persistence-after-stopping cannot be expressed in a
(drug, event, quarter) contingency table with no denominator and no coded term, so a flat or generic
signal is **not** reassurance about PSSD — it is a demonstration of a coding/definitional blind spot.
Distinguish this cleanly from the queued *sevoflurane* pieces: there the blind spot is a
genetically-defined subpopulation diluted to invisibility; here the blind spot is that the outcome
*definition itself* (persistent, off-drug) does not exist in the dictionary and the temporal
structure defeats the method. The transferable rule: before trusting a disproportionality result,
ask whether the harm you care about is even *representable* as a PT on-treatment.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** The coded sexual-dysfunction PTs are common and the
antidepressants are high-volume, so the measurable analysis is straightforward. The article's value
is conceptual (the representability argument) rather than in data wrangling — a strong
methodology-lane fit. Careful, non-clinical framing throughout (no causal claim about PSSD).

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Xu, Liu, Yang, Kong, "Differential signal strength of male sexual dysfunction
  associated with antidepressants: a disproportionality analysis of FAERS database," *Sex Med*
  2026;14(5):qfag082 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/sexmed/qfag082)
- PSSD Network, "Regulator Statements" (EMA 2019 product-information update; US fluoxetine-only
  label; Nov 2025 MedDRA standalone-term rejection) — https://www.pssdnetwork.org/regulator-statements
- RxISK, "Post-SSRI Sexual Dysfunction (PSSD)" (clinical description; persistence after

## 5. A within-class kidney signal where reverse causation, not the drug, may be doing the work

**Working title:** "Anticoagulant-Related Nephropathy in FAERS: Dabigatran vs the Factor-Xa
Inhibitors, and the Protopathic Trap"

**Safety question.** "Anticoagulant-related nephropathy" (ARN) — acute kidney injury from glomerular
hemorrhage and tubular obstruction under anticoagulation — is a recognized entity, first described
with warfarin and reported most often with the direct thrombin inhibitor dabigatran. A new (Sept
2026) FAERS comparative study contrasts direct thrombin inhibitors against factor-Xa inhibitors
(apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban) for kidney injury. But the DOACs are a textbook setting for
*reverse causation / protopathic bias*: renal function drives DOAC choice and dosing (dabigatran is
heavily renally cleared and contraindicated in poor renal function), and AKI itself provokes
bleeding, dose changes, and reporting. Can the site show both the within-class contrast *and* why the
naive "dabigatran → AKI" reading is exactly the kind of confounded signal its methods section warns
about?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Chen, Shi, Li, Wang, Guo, Wu, "Kidney injury associated
with direct oral anticoagulants: a comparative study of direct thrombin inhibitors and factor Xa
inhibitors based on literature and database analysis," *Renal Failure* 2026;48(1):2725468, published
**September 10, 2026** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/0886022X.2026.2725468)), is a fresh
within-class DOAC comparison — the DOACs are among the most-prescribed drugs in the world, so a "how
to read this correctly" piece has broad reach. It extends the site's existing gotcha catalogue
(**protopathic / reverse-causation bias**; **confounding by indication**) with a concrete,
current example and a clean active-comparator design.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) Four-method engine for **dabigatran** vs the
factor-Xa inhibitors (**apixaban**, **rivaroxaban**, **edoxaban**) against a renal PT cluster —
*Acute kidney injury*, *Renal haemorrhage*, *Haematuria*, *Nephropathy toxic*, *Renal failure* —
with observed counts and the quarterly caterpillar. (2) The methodology payload: an active
within-class comparator is the right frame (all four share the anticoagulant indication), but even
that does not defeat *channelling* — dabigatran's renal clearance means sicker-kidney patients are
selected off it, or onto reduced doses, so a difference in AKI reporting may reflect *who gets which
drug*, not the drug's nephrotoxicity. Layer the protopathic caveat: AKI → hematuria/bleeding →
reporting can run backwards through the causal arrow. The transferable rule: a within-class
comparison controls the indication but not the *channelling on the organ at issue* — here, the
kidney. Explicit what-FAERS-can/cannot-show throughout.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** All four DOACs are extremely high-volume with standard
renal PTs, so coverage is trivial. The genuine work is honest treatment of renal-function channelling
(unobservable in FAERS) and the protopathic direction — which is the article's methods content, not a
nuisance to hide.

  anticoagulants: a comparative study of direct thrombin inhibitors and factor Xa inhibitors based on
  literature and database analysis," *Ren Fail* 2026;48(1):2725468 —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/0886022X.2026.2725468)
- Ryan M, et al., "Warfarin-related nephropathy is the tip of the iceberg: direct thrombin inhibitor
