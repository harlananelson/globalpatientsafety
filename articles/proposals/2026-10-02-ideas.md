## 1. Replicate it somewhere else, or it isn't a signal — macrolide cardiotoxicity in two independent databases

**Working title:** "Two Databases, One Signal: Why Azithromycin's Heart Risk Is Believable and
Most FAERS Leaderboards Aren't"

**Safety question.** QT prolongation with macrolides is textbook and label-recognised. A new
study pushes further — claiming azithromycin-specific heart-*failure* signals and
clarithromycin-specific *ischemic* signals beyond the familiar QT/torsade story — and, crucially,
tests whether those signals reproduce in a *second, independent* national database (Japan's
JADER) rather than resting on FAERS alone. The methodological question for the site: what does
it mean that a signal survives in two unrelated spontaneous-reporting systems, and why is
cross-database concordance a far stronger defence against artifact than any single-database
EB05, no matter how large?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Zhou L. et al., "Cardiotoxicity of macrolide
antibiotics: a pharmacovigilance study integrating FAERS and JADER databases," *Naunyn
Schmiedebergs Arch Pharmacol*, published **September 26, 2026** (FAERS and JADER, 2016Q1–2025Q4),
analysed 42,605 macrolide reports in FAERS and 4,613 in JADER. They report erythromycin's long-QT
ROR at 81.8 (95% CI 45.1–148.5), azithromycin at 34.7, and clarithromycin at 18.1 — with the
azithromycin and clarithromycin QT signals *independently confirmed in JADER* (ROR 24.4 and
13.2). They also report azithromycin-specific heart-failure signals and clarithromycin-specific
ischemic signals, median time-to-onset of 1 day, and ~10% case fatality among cardiac-AE reports
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05949-8)). Azithromycin is one of the most-dispensed
antibiotics in the US, so a careful "how to tell a real signal from a leaderboard artifact" piece
has broad reach and a clean regulatory backdrop (FDA's 2013 azithromycin QT communication).

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Run the four-method engine for azithromycin,
clarithromycin, and erythromycin against a cardiac PT cluster — *Long QT syndrome*, *Torsade de
pointes*, *Electrocardiogram QT prolonged*, *Cardiac failure*, *Myocardial ischaemia*,
*Ventricular arrhythmia* — with observed counts beside every EB05 and the quarterly caterpillar.
The methodological centerpiece is *replication-as-robustness*: the QT signal is the positive
control (known biology, should be rock-solid and well-populated); the heart-failure/ischemic
extensions are the hypotheses to probe. Discuss honestly that we have one database (FAERS), so
we can reproduce the paper's FAERS arm but not its JADER arm — and use that limitation to teach
*why* the paper's two-database design is stronger than ours, and what a reader should demand
before believing a novel single-database signal. Pairs naturally with the site's existing "raw
leaderboard lies" gotcha: a huge ROR on one database is a hypothesis; the same signal on two
unrelated databases is evidence.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low.** All three macrolides are high-volume in FAERS with deep
coverage and standard cardiac PTs; the QT cluster is trivially populated. The only real work is
a defensible PT cluster and honest framing of the single-database limitation (which is itself the
article's methods payload).

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Zhou L, Zhou Y, Liu C, et al., "Cardiotoxicity of macrolide antibiotics: a
  pharmacovigilance study integrating FAERS and JADER databases," *Naunyn Schmiedebergs Arch
  Pharmacol* 2026 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05949-8)
- FDA Drug Safety Communication (2013), "Azithromycin (Zithromax or Zmax) and the risk of
  potentially fatal heart rhythms" —
  https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-drug-safety-communication-azithromycin-zithromax-or-zmax-and-risk-potentially-fatal-heart

## 2. "88% of these drugs don't warn about it" — a provocative stat that confounding by indication quietly destroys

**Working title:** "Does the Drug Cause the Bipolar Disorder, or Does the Bipolar Disorder Call
for the Drug? Reading a FAERS 'Drug-Induced Bipolar' Leaderboard"

**Safety question.** A new FAERS study screens the whole database for drugs disproportionately
reported with bipolar disorder and lands on an attention-grabbing headline: 88% of the top 50
drugs carry *no* bipolar-risk warning in their label. But the top of that leaderboard is thick
with agents that are *prescribed to treat* bipolar disorder, mood instability, or their
comorbidities — naltrexone, oxcarbazepine, antipsychotics. This is the site's confounding-by-
indication and protopathic/reverse-causation gotchas in their purest psychiatric form: the
adverse event *is the indication*, or the early symptoms of it. The question: how much of a
"drug-induced bipolar" leaderboard is real signal, and how much is the diagnosis that led to the
prescription showing up as an "adverse event"?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Du P. et al., "Drug-associated bipolar disorder: A
disproportionality analysis of the FAERS database," *Psychiatry Res*, published **August 11,
2026** (FAERS 2004Q1–2025Q3; MedDRA v27.1), analysed 23.6M reports, found 17,620 bipolar-disorder
cases, with varenicline the single most-reported drug (537 cases) and the top RORs going to
pizotifen, tafenoquine, flupenthixol, naltrexone, and pemoline. The authors report that 44 of the
top 50 drugs (88%) lack any bipolar-risk mention in their labels, and note sex- and subtype-
specific patterns (naltrexone→BD-I, oxcarbazepine→BD-II) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117375)).
That "88% unlabelled" framing is exactly the kind of number that travels — and exactly the kind
the site is built to contextualise rather than amplify.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Reproduce the screen for a handful of the
paper's top drugs against a bipolar PT cluster (*Bipolar disorder*, *Bipolar I disorder*,
*Bipolar II disorder*, *Mania*, *Hypomania*) with observed counts and the caterpillar. The
teaching payload is the sorting: (1) agents where the indication *is* mood disorder or where the
drug treats bipolar/its comorbidities (oxcarbazepine, naltrexone for mood/substance use,
antipsychotics) — classic confounding by indication and reverse causation, where a label warning
would be nonsensical; (2) agents with a genuine neuropsychiatric mechanism and a real regulatory
history (varenicline, whose Chantix neuropsychiatric boxed warning was added in 2009 and removed
in 2016 after the EAGLES trial) — a natural-experiment date to check the trajectory against; and
(3) small-N high-ROR curiosities (pizotifen, tafenoquine) where the number is a leaderboard
artifact. The transferable lesson: a disproportionality leaderboard of a *psychiatric* outcome is
dominated by why the drug was prescribed, and "X% of drugs lack a label warning" is a statement
about confounding, not about missing warnings.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** Varenicline, naltrexone, oxcarbazepine, and the
antipsychotics are well-populated in FAERS; the bipolar PT cluster is standard. The real work is
the honest taxonomy of confounding-by-indication vs mechanism vs small-N — which is the article.

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Du P, Chen X, Zhang W, Zhou Y, Xu Y, "Drug-associated bipolar disorder: A
  disproportionality analysis of the FAERS database," *Psychiatry Res* 2026;365:117375 —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117375)
- Anthenelli RM et al., "Neuropsychiatric safety and efficacy of varenicline, bupropion, and
  nicotine patch in smokers with and without psychiatric disorders (EAGLES)," *Lancet* 2016
  (the trial behind the 2016 removal of the varenicline neuropsychiatric boxed warning) —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30272-0)

## 3. The signal that only appears when you split by sex — allopurinol, febuxostat, and effect modification

**Working title:** "Pool the Sexes and You Lose the Signal: Sex-Stratified Disproportionality for
Gout Drugs, and What FAERS Still Can't See"

**Safety question.** A new study runs allopurinol and febuxostat (xanthine oxidase inhibitors)
through FAERS *separately within male and female reports* and finds sex-specific signals that a
pooled analysis would blur: allopurinol→pancytopenia concentrated in women, febuxostat→
rhabdomyolysis concentrated in men. It then checks those signals for directional consistency in a
second database (EudraVigilance). The methodology question: when is a pooled EB05 actively
misleading because sex is an effect modifier — and, as a second theme, where does even a correct
sex-stratified FAERS signal hit a hard wall (allopurinol's severe hypersensitivity is driven by
the *HLA-B\*58:01* allele, which FAERS cannot see — the same "structurally invisible subpopulation"
lesson as the sevoflurane idea, but for a pharmacogenomic rather than ancestry variable)?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Chen Y. et al., "Sex-stratified disproportionality
signals of adverse events with allopurinol and febuxostat: a retrospective pharmacovigilance
analysis of FAERS and EudraVigilance," *Clin Rheumatol*, published **September 30, 2026** (FAERS
2004Q1–2024Q4), computed RORs separately by sex and reported allopurinol→pancytopenia in women
(ROR 6.87, 95% CI 6.09–7.75, n=271) and febuxostat→rhabdomyolysis in men (ROR 4.55, 95% CI
3.64–5.68, n=79), with directionally consistent patterns in EudraVigilance
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10067-026-08452-w)). The regulatory backdrop is concrete:
febuxostat carries an FDA boxed warning for cardiovascular death added in 2019 after the CARES
trial, and allopurinol hypersensitivity has a well-established HLA-B\*58:01 pharmacogenomic basis —
both give the piece real anchors.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Run the four-method engine for allopurinol and
febuxostat against the relevant clusters (*Pancytopenia*, *Agranulocytosis*, *DRESS* for
allopurinol; *Rhabdomyolysis*, *Blood creatine phosphokinase increased* for febuxostat), first
pooled and then sex-stratified, showing how the pooled EB05 can sit below or near threshold while
a sex-specific one clears it — a clean demonstration that stratification is not optional when an
effect is sex-modified. Observed counts beside every EB05; caterpillar for the well-populated
signals. Second theme, stated plainly: even a correct sex-stratified signal cannot reach the
*genotype* layer — allopurinol's most dangerous reaction tracks HLA-B\*58:01, invisible to
spontaneous reporting — so FAERS can tell you *that* and *in whom by sex*, never *in whom by
allele*. Honest caveats: differential reporting by sex, and confounding by indication (gout
demographics skew male).

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** Allopurinol and febuxostat are high-volume with
deep FAERS histories; the clusters are standard. Sex-stratification is a straightforward split.
We can reproduce the FAERS arm but not the EudraVigilance arm (single-database limitation — same
honest note as idea #1).

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Chen Y, Lu W, Tan L, et al., "Sex-stratified disproportionality signals of
  adverse events with allopurinol and febuxostat: a retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis of
  FAERS and EudraVigilance," *Clin Rheumatol* 2026 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10067-026-08452-w)
- White WB et al., "Cardiovascular Safety of Febuxostat or Allopurinol in Patients with Gout
  (CARES)," *N Engl J Med* 2018 (the trial behind the 2019 febuxostat CV boxed warning) —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1710895)

## 4. A boxed warning built on a lab value — Injectafer, hypophosphatemia, and the outcome FAERS struggles to see

**Working title:** "The Warning Is About a Blood Test: Why FAERS Hears Hypophosphatemia Loudly and
the Broken Bones Barely at All"

**Safety question.** On September 1, 2026 the FDA added a *boxed* warning to ferric carboxymaltose
(Injectafer) for symptomatic hypophosphatemia — the strongest label action, arriving after a 2020
warning and a 2025 revision failed to stop the reports. Hypophosphatemia is a lab abnormality with
a loud, easily reported acute presentation (weakness, fatigue, in severe cases arrhythmia or
seizure); its feared *chronic* consequence — osteomalacia and insufficiency fractures — is
delayed, under-recognised, and rarely attributed back to an iron infusion months earlier. This is
the site's "the rare severe outcome is invisible; the mechanism is loud" gotcha attached to a
brand-new, dated regulatory action: can FAERS see the hypophosphatemia signal clearly, and does
the downstream bone outcome lag and under-report exactly as the mechanism predicts?

**Why it matters now.** The regulatory event is this-month and concrete: on **September 1, 2026**
FDA added a boxed warning to Injectafer's labeling for symptomatic hypophosphatemia, recommending
phosphate monitoring for at-risk patients and for anyone receiving a repeat course within three
months — escalating from the February 2020 warning and a 2025 revision because reports continued
despite them. Ferric carboxymaltose is also now FDA-approved in pediatric iron-deficiency anemia,
widening exposure. This is an unusually clean regulator-action-plus-mechanism pairing for a FAERS
piece, and it extends the carbidopa/levodopa B6 article's template (warning built on a mechanism
whose loud signal long predates the formal action).

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Four-method engine for ferric carboxymaltose
against a two-tier cluster: the *acute/mechanism* tier (*Hypophosphataemia*, *Blood phosphorus
decreased*, *Fatigue*, *Muscular weakness*) vs the *downstream/skeletal* tier (*Osteomalacia*,
*Bone pain*, *Pathological fracture*, *Stress fracture*). Observed counts beside every EB05 and
the quarterly caterpillar, with the February-2020 and 2025 label dates as natural-experiment
markers to test whether reporting of the *acute* signal tracks the label changes (stimulated
reporting) while the *skeletal* tier stays sparse and flat. The transferable lesson: a boxed
warning about a *lab value* is reportable and visible; the clinical endpoint that makes the lab
value matter (fragility fractures, months later, attributed to anything but an iron infusion) is
structurally under-captured by spontaneous reporting — so a strong acute signal and a near-absent
chronic one is exactly what the mechanism predicts, not reassurance. Compare, as a class check,
whether the liver-sparing iron products (e.g., iron sucrose, ferric derisomaltose) show the same
phosphate signal or not.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** Ferric carboxymaltose is well-represented in FAERS
and *Hypophosphataemia* is a standard PT, so the acute tier is easy. The skeletal tier will be
small-N by design — which is the point — and requires care not to over-read the handful of
osteomalacia/fracture reports.

  Injection (Injectafer) to Describe Risk of Low Phosphate Levels" (September 1, 2026) —
  https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-adds-boxed-warning-labeling-ferric-carboxymaltose-injection-injectafer-describe-risk-low
- Renal & Urology News, "Injectafer Label Updated With Boxed Warning for Low Phosphate Risk"

## 5. "Potential unlisted signal" on 33 reports — small-N skepticism in a pediatric dupilumab screen

**Working title:** "Vitiligo, ROR 10, n = 33: How to Read an 'Unlisted Signal' Before You Believe
It"

**Safety question.** A large pediatric FAERS screen of dupilumab flags a cluster of eye-catching
"potential unlisted signals" — vitiligo, skin depigmentation, even *eye colour change* — at
double-digit RORs resting on a few dozen reports each, alongside the expected, well-populated,
label-consistent ones (rash, injection-site pain). This is the site's "small-N EB05 needs the
trajectory before it means anything" gotcha in a sympathetic, high-interest setting (a widely used
pediatric biologic). The question: given FAERS data limitations, which of these is a hypothesis
worth a registry and which is a leaderboard mirage — and how does a reader tell the difference
*from the disproportionality output alone*?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Ma X. et al., "Pharmacovigilance signals of dupilumab
among pediatric patients: A real-world analysis based on the FAERS database," *Hum Vaccin
Immunother*, published **September 4, 2026** (FAERS 2017–2025; 44,593 pediatric reports with
dupilumab as primary suspect), reported the core signals rash (n=4,105; ROR 4.55), injection-site
pain (n=3,582; ROR 8.59), and skin exfoliation (n=1,834; ROR 3.41), plus "potential unlisted
signals" vitiligo (n=33; ROR 10.46, 95% CI 6.52–16.78), skin depigmentation (n=32; ROR 13.04), and
eye colour change (n=13; ROR 7.06), with most AEs onset within 30 days (median 14) and the authors
explicitly framing the associations as hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/21645515.2026.2727805)). Dupilumab's expanding pediatric use makes a
careful "how to read an unlisted signal" piece timely, and the paper's own honest framing is a gift
for the site's voice.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Reproduce the pediatric dupilumab screen with
the four-method engine, putting the *well-populated* signals (rash, injection-site pain —
thousands of reports, stable caterpillar, trustworthy) directly beside the *small-N* curiosities
(vitiligo, depigmentation, eye-colour-change — dozens of reports, wide CIs, no meaningful
trajectory). The teaching payload is a reproducible checklist for reading any "unlisted signal":
observed count, CI width, whether ≥2 of 4 methods agree, whether the quarterly trajectory is
populated or a single-quarter spike, and whether a plausible pediatric-specific reporting channel
(parental reporting, specialty registries, media attention to depigmentation) could inflate it.
Note the known biological thread honestly but without a causal claim — dupilumab-associated
depigmentation/vitiligo has case-report support, which is exactly why it is a *hypothesis* worth
stating and not yet a conclusion. Standard pediatric caveats: differential reporting, indication
(atopic dermatitis itself affects skin), and the impossibility of incidence from spontaneous data.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** Dupilumab is very high-volume in FAERS and the
pediatric subset is large; the core signals are trivially populated. The small-N signals are, by
construction, a handful of reports — easy to pull, and the care not to over-read them is the
article's methods content.

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Ma X, Fan J, Liu B, "Pharmacovigilance signals of dupilumab among pediatric
  patients: A real-world analysis based on the FAERS database," *Hum Vaccin Immunother*
  2026;22(1):2727805 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/21645515.2026.2727805)
- According to PubMed: a companion pediatric FAERS screen using the same four-method design —
  Fang W, Liu B, Cai L, "Real-world pharmacovigilance of rituximab-associated adverse reactions in
  children and adolescents," *Hum Vaccin Immunother* 2026;22(1):2731825 (method/limitations
  precedent for pediatric FAERS screens and indication-bias caveats) —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/21645515.2026.2731825)
