# Article Ideas — Week of 2026-08-21

Four data-driven article proposals grounded in pharmacovigilance developments from the
last ~1–2 months, matched to what this site already does: independent disproportionality
reanalysis of FAERS/VAERS using the four-method engine (GPS/EBGM, PRR, ROR, BCPNN/IC),
EB05-thresholded signals with ≥2 methods agreeing, latest-quarter snapshot **plus**
quarterly caterpillar trajectory, observed counts shown beside every EB05.

**Editorial lane (per `ARTICLE_QUEUE.md`):** methodology first. Harlan is a statistician,
not an MD, so each piece is framed as a transferable "reading FDA adverse-event data" lesson
anchored to a concrete drug example — *not* a clinical causation claim. Voice reference: the
Christine Cotton VAERS piece — modest, testable claims; explicit about what disproportionality
can and cannot show; careful with stimulated-reporting, litigation-cluster, channelling, and
confounding-by-indication caveats.

**These are proposals only.** No analyses have been run and no numbers below are computed from
our data — every quantitative statement is sourced to a regulator or the peer-reviewed

each article is to reproduce (or fail to reproduce) them in our own four-method engine.

## 1. Most-reported ≠ strongest signal: montelukast, varenicline, and sleep disorders

**Working title:** "The Drug at the Top of the List Isn't the Signal: Report Volume vs.
Disproportionality in FAERS"

**Safety question.** When a large FAERS screen of drug-induced sleep disorders is run, the drug
with the *most reports* and the drug with the *strongest disproportionality signal* are two
different drugs. Which one should a reader worry about — and why does conflating "most reported"
with "most disproportionate" mislead almost everyone who reads a pharmacovigilance table?

**Why it matters now.** A global pharmacovigilance profiling of drug-induced sleep disorders
published **28 July 2026** (Zhang et al., *Sleep Medicine*) analyzed 21 years of reports (>22
million records; 411,736 sleep-disorder reports) with all four disproportionality algorithms
(ROR, PRR-χ², BCPNN, MGPS) requiring simultaneous positivity. **Varenicline accounted for the
most reports (n = 16,200), yet montelukast showed the strongest signal (ROR 11.78, 95% CI
11.40–12.18).** The paper also flags that ~1 in 5 of the 131 signalling drugs carry no FDA
labelling for the effect. Montelukast is a natural anchor because FDA already placed a **boxed
warning for serious neuropsychiatric events on montelukast on 3 March 2020** — so its sleep
signal is a *known* effect the data should recover, making it an honest yardstick against which
to read the report-volume-vs-signal-strength distinction.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Run GPS/EBGM + PRR + ROR + BCPNN for
montelukast and varenicline against a sleep-disorder PT cluster (*Insomnia*, *Sleep disorder*,
*Abnormal dreams*, *Nightmare*, *Somnambulism*, *Middle insomnia*). The methodological centerpiece
is a single scatter/two-axis figure: **report count on one axis, EB05 on the other**, showing the
two are nearly orthogonal — varenicline sits far right (high volume) but low-to-moderate EB05,
montelukast far up (high EB05) on fewer reports. Show observed counts beside every EB05 and a
quarterly caterpillar so the montelukast signal's stability (a labelled, real effect) contrasts
with volume-driven noise. Transferable lesson for the "Reading FDA Data" series: **a leaderboard
ranked by report count answers a different question than one ranked by disproportionality**, and
newsrooms routinely quote the wrong one.

**Expected data difficulty.** *Easy.* Both drugs and the sleep-disorder PT cluster are
well-populated in FAERS; no exotic MedDRA mapping or rare-event small-N problem.

## 2. The active-comparator trick: cholinesterase inhibitors vs. memantine

**Working title:** "Whose Denominator? Using a Same-Indication Comparator to Strip Out
Channelling Bias"

**Safety question.** Nearly every FAERS study computes disproportionality against *the whole
database* as the background. But for a drug used only in one narrow, sick population, "the rest of
FAERS" is the wrong comparison group — it bakes in channelling and confounding-by-indication. What
happens to a candidate signal when you swap the background for a **same-indication, different-
mechanism active comparator** instead? A newly published dementia example makes the technique
concrete.

**Why it matters now.** A FAERS disproportionality study published **17 August 2026** (Sayion,
*Naunyn-Schmiedeberg's Archives of Pharmacology*) tested whether sexual disinhibition — which
appears only as scattered case reports and is largely absent from product labels — is
disproportionately reported for cholinesterase inhibitors (CHEIs: donepezil, rivastigmine,
galantamine). Crucially, it used **memantine, a same-indication dementia drug of distinct
mechanism, as the active comparator** (47 quarters, 2014Q3–2026Q1, ~15.9M de-duplicated reports),
with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control, negative controls, and READUS-PV-compliant reporting. Each
CHEI-vs-memantine contrast was concordant (donepezil ROR 5.16, rivastigmine 5.07, galantamine
6.18; pooled class 5.58), and the signal survived excluding memantine-co-reported cases. It is a
clean, current worked example of the design choice that most FAERS papers skip.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Reproduce the analysis in our four-method engine
two ways and put them side by side: (a) CHEIs vs. **whole-database** background, and (b) CHEIs vs.
**memantine** active comparator. The teaching payload is the *difference* between the two — how the
active-comparator design changes EB05/ROR and what that implies about how much of a naïve signal is
channelling. Add negative-control PTs (events with no plausible link) to show specificity, and
show observed counts beside every estimate. This directly extends the site's active-comparator
thread (the vamorolone-vs-deflazacort piece) but generalizes it into a reusable method lesson:
**the comparator group is a modelling decision, not a given, and it can make or break a signal.**
Frame explicitly as hypothesis-generating — sexual disinhibition is unlabelled and this cannot
establish incidence or causation.

**Expected data difficulty.** *Moderate.* Requires assembling the memantine comparator cohort and a
defensible sexual-disinhibition / impulse-reward PT list; CHEI and memantine reports are plentiful,
but the target PTs are sparse, so small-N EB05 caveats and the trajectory check matter.

**Citations.**
- Sayion K. "Cholinesterase inhibitors and sexual disinhibition in dementia: a FAERS
  disproportionality study." *Naunyn Schmiedebergs Arch Pharmacol.* 2026 Aug 17.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05748-1) *(via PubMed, PMID 42604874)*
- READUS-PV reporting guideline for disproportionality analyses (the standard the paper follows) —
  cite for the methods framing: Fusaroli M, et al. "The REporting of A Disproportionality analysis
  for drUg Safety signal detection using individual case safety reports in PharmacoVigilance
  (READUS-PV)." *Drug Saf.* 2024. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-024-01421-9

## 3. FAERS isn't only side effects: medication errors and therapeutic failure in long-acting injectables

**Working title:** "The Signals That Aren't Side Effects: Reading Product-Use and Therapeutic-
Failure Reports in FAERS"

**Safety question.** A FAERS report can describe a classic adverse reaction — but it can also
describe a *missed dose*, a *wrong-interval injection*, an *administration error*, or outright
*therapeutic failure*. For long-acting injectables, where adherence to the injection schedule
essentially *is* the efficacy, those "non-clinical" report categories may be the safety story that
matters most. Does disproportionality analysis restricted to product-use and therapeutic-failure
terms surface a real, actionable signal that a side-effect-only screen would miss entirely?

**Why it matters now.** A FAERS disproportionality analysis of injectable **Cabenuva**
(cabotegravir/rilpivirine, the first complete long-acting injectable HIV regimen), published
**14 August 2026** (Li et al., *Journal of Public Health Research*), found that alongside expected
injection-site reactions, the prominent reported terms were **product dose omission, inappropriate
schedule of product administration, product administration error, and therapeutic failure /
disease-progression terms** — implementation problems, not pharmacology. This lands exactly as the
long-acting-injectable field expands: FDA approved twice-yearly **lenacapavir (Yeztugo)** for HIV
PrEP on **18 June 2025**, making adherence-as-scheduling a growing safety surface across a whole
product class.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Run GPS/EBGM + PRR + ROR + BCPNN for Cabenuva
restricted to two PT families most FAERS screens discard: **(a) medication-error / product-use
issues** (*Product dose omission issue*, *Inappropriate schedule of product administration*,
*Product administration error*, *Wrong technique in product usage process*) and **(b) therapeutic
failure** (*Drug ineffective*, *Therapeutic response decreased*, *Disease progression*). Compare
against an oral-ART active comparator to show the pattern is specific to the injectable delivery
model, not to the molecules. Latest-quarter snapshot + caterpillar + observed counts throughout.
The transferable lesson: **the MedDRA System Organ Class you filter to is an editorial choice**;
excluding "product issues" and "therapeutic failure" — as most disproportionality papers silently
do — can erase the dominant real-world risk of an entire delivery format. A genuinely novel
dimension for the site, and increasingly relevant as LAIs proliferate.

**Expected data difficulty.** *Moderate.* Cabenuva volume is modest but adequate; the work is in
curating defensible medication-error and therapeutic-failure PT groupings and an oral-ART
comparator cohort. Route/administration fields in FAERS are inconsistently populated — a caveat
worth stating, and itself part of the lesson.

**Citations.**
- Li J, et al. "Post-marketing safety signals, therapeutic failure, and product-use issues reported
  with injectable Cabenuva: A FAERS disproportionality analysis." *J Public Health Res.* 2026 Aug
  14;15(3). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/22799036261480270) *(via PubMed, PMID 42605295)*
- Gilead newsroom, 18 Jun 2025: "Yeztugo (lenacapavir) Is Now the First and Only FDA-Approved HIV
  Prevention Option Offering 6 Months of Protection."
  https://www.gilead.com/news/news-details/2025/yeztugo-lenacapavir-is-now-the-first-and-only-fda-approved-hiv-prevention-option-offering-6-months-of-protection
- Independent confirmation of the 18 Jun 2025 approval: JAMA, "FDA Approves First Twice-Yearly PrEP
  for Long-Acting HIV Prevention." https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2836644

## 4. Time-to-onset as a plausibility filter: a small bisphosphonate–glaucoma signal

**Working title:** "How Long After the First Dose? Time-to-Onset as a Cheap Plausibility Check on a
Small Signal"

**Safety question.** A disproportionality signal on a few hundred reports is easy to over-read. One
under-used, almost-free sanity check is the **time-to-onset distribution**: if the same drug class
reports a supposed effect anywhere from a week to four years after starting, is that a coherent
biological signal or a grab-bag of unrelated reporting? A newly published bisphosphonate–glaucoma
analysis is a compact test bed for the technique — and for the small-N humility the site keeps
preaching.

**Why it matters now.** A FAERS pharmacovigilance analysis published **12 August 2026** (Zhang et
al., *Canadian Journal of Ophthalmology*) reported disproportionality signals linking specific
bisphosphonates to glaucoma subtypes — alendronic acid (ROR 2.42) and pamidronic acid (ROR 2.23)
at the group level, with subtype signals such as pamidronate–open-angle glaucoma (ROR 8.62) — on a
total of just **539 reports**, and, tellingly, with **median time-to-onset ranging from 10 days
(risedronate) to 1579 days (pamidronate).** That ~150-fold spread in onset time across one drug
class, on a small report base, is exactly the situation where an EB05 headline needs a plausibility
filter before anyone believes it. Ophthalmic safety is also live territory for this site given the
existing GLP-1→NAION eye-signal work.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Reproduce the class in our four-method engine
(alendronate, pamidronate, risedronate, zoledronate × glaucoma-subtype PTs), but make the
**time-to-onset distribution the centerpiece, not the EB05**: plot onset histograms / Weibull shape
per drug, show how a wildly dispersed or implausibly-short onset undercuts a signal that the point
estimate alone would sell. Pair with the site's standard small-N discipline — observed counts beside
every EB05, the quarterly caterpillar, and an explicit "how few reports is this really" callout.
Transferable lesson: **time-to-onset is a near-free second axis that most disproportionality
write-ups omit**, and it routinely deflates (or, occasionally, strengthens) a borderline signal that
a bare ROR would leave ambiguous.

**Expected data difficulty.** *Moderate-to-hard.* The catch is the lesson: FAERS onset dates require
both event-onset and therapy-start fields, which are frequently missing, so the computable
time-to-onset subset is much smaller than the report count — a limitation to quantify and foreground
rather than hide. Glaucoma-subtype PTs are sparse, so this is squarely a small-N piece.

**Citations.**
- Zhang Y, et al. "Bisphosphonates and glaucoma: a pharmacovigilance signal detection analysis using
  the FAERS database." *Can J Ophthalmol.* 2026 Aug 12.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcjo.2026.06.019) *(via PubMed, PMID 42498257)*
- For the time-to-onset / Weibull shape-parameter method as applied in a same-week FAERS paper (use
  as method precedent): Zhang M, et al. *Sleep Med.* 2026 (Weibull β time-to-onset profiling).
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2026.109161) *(via PubMed, PMID 42551056)*

## Quick ranking (for triage)

1. **#1 Most-reported ≠ strongest signal (montelukast/varenicline).** Highest-value, lowest-risk:
   easy data, a clean single-figure lesson, a labelled anchor effect to validate against, and a
   gotcha (report volume vs. disproportionality) not yet covered in the queue.
2. **#2 Active-comparator trick (CHEIs vs. memantine).** Strong methods payload squarely in the
   site's lane; extends the vamorolone active-comparator thread into a reusable design lesson.
   Sparse target PTs are the main caveat.
3. **#3 Non-side-effect signals in long-acting injectables (Cabenuva).** Genuinely novel dimension
   for the site and timely with the LAI expansion; slightly more curation work on PT groupings.
4. **#4 Time-to-onset plausibility filter (bisphosphonates–glaucoma).** Good, self-contained method
   lesson, but the missing-onset-date problem makes it the hardest data lift and the smallest N —
   best as a shorter "technique" piece than a flagship.
