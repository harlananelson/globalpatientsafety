# Article Ideas — Week of 2026-10-09

Four data-driven article proposals grounded in pharmacovigilance developments from the last
~1–2 months (all four have a **September–October 2026** regulatory or literature hook), matched
to what this site already does: independent disproportionality reanalysis of FAERS/VAERS using
the four-method engine (GPS/EBGM, PRR, ROR, BCPNN/IC), EB05-thresholded signals with ≥2 methods
agreeing, latest-quarter snapshot **plus** quarterly caterpillar trajectory, observed counts
shown beside every EB05.

**Editorial lane (per `ARTICLE_QUEUE.md`):** methodology first. Harlan is a statistician, not an
MD, so each piece is framed as a transferable "reading FDA adverse-event data" lesson anchored to
a concrete drug/vaccine example — *not* a clinical causation claim. Voice reference: the Christine
Cotton VAERS piece — modest, testable claims; explicit about what disproportionality can and
cannot show; careful with stimulated-reporting, litigation-cluster, and confounding caveats.

**These are proposals only.** No analyses have been run and no numbers below are computed from our
data — every quantitative statement is sourced to a regulator or the peer-reviewed literature, not
to our parquet. Numbers from published papers are *their* numbers; the point of each article is to
reproduce (or fail to reproduce) them in our own four-method engine.

**Deduplication check.** These avoid the current queue (Zostavax-vs-Shingrix zoster, GLP-1→NAION
survivability, AEMS reproducibility, Trop-2 ADC), the 2026-07-24 batch (sevoflurane invisible
subpopulation, atorvastatin huge-PRR-on-tiny-N, teprotumumab hearing loss, vamorolone-vs-deflazacort,

## 1. The huge disproportionality number that was real: a brand-new boxed warning on a signal our engine would have screamed about for years

**Working title:** "When PRR = 500 Is *Not* an Artifact: Injectafer, Hypophosphatemia, and the
Boxed Warning That Caught Up to the Data"

**Safety question.** On September 1, 2026 the FDA added a **boxed warning** to ferric carboxymaltose
(Injectafer) for symptomatic hypophosphatemia — a signal that sits at the very top of the FAERS
leaderboard (a published FAERS analysis put the hypophosphatemia PRR at **520.7**, IC 8.0). The
site's running gotcha catalogue says "the raw leaderboard lies" — top-EB05 pairs are usually
confounding, route, litigation, or definitional artifacts. So this is the sharp test of that rule:
here is a top-of-leaderboard signal that is **not** an artifact, is mechanistically clean
(IV iron → FGF23 → renal phosphate wasting), and has just been promoted to the most serious label
warning FDA issues. Can our four-method engine reproduce the enormous signal, and — more importantly
— can we articulate *why* this particular giant number deserved belief when most do not (large N,
dose/repeat-exposure relationship, a specific lab-to-outcome mechanism, years of a stable
trajectory), i.e. the features that separate a real extreme signal from a small-N mirage?

**Why it matters now.** The FDA Drug Safety Communication is dated **September 1, 2026**; FDA states
its assessment drew on FAERS (now part of the AEMS database), published literature, and the Sentinel
System, and notes serious harm (fractures, hospitalization) continued despite earlier label updates
in 2020, 2023, and 2025 — a multi-year lag between a loud signal and the boxed warning that is itself
the methodological story. A concurrent EMA/PRAC review of injectable iron (hypophosphataemia and bone
effects) was reported the same month. Injectafer is high-volume, so this reaches a large audience and
directly extends the site's AEMS/"reading FDA data" lane.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) Run GPS/EBGM + PRR + ROR + BCPNN for ferric
carboxymaltose against *Hypophosphataemia* and the downstream skeletal cluster (*Osteomalacia*,
*Bone pain*, *Pathological fracture*, *Hypophosphataemic osteomalacia*, *Blood phosphorus decreased*),
with observed counts beside every EB05. (2) The teaching centerpiece — the **mechanism-is-loud,
rare-outcome-is-quiet** gotcha in reverse: hypophosphatemia (the lab intermediate) is one of the
loudest signals in all of FAERS, while the outcome that drove the boxed warning (fractures/osteomalacia)
is comparatively sparse — plot both trajectories and show the lab signal led the fracture reports by
years. (3) The **natural-experiment** check (as in the NAION piece): overlay the 2020/2023/2025 label
dates and the Sept 2026 boxed warning on the quarterly caterpillar and ask whether the signal was
already decisive long before the regulator acted. (4) Optional class contrast: other IV irons
(iron sucrose, ferric derisomaltose) to show the signal tracks the specific product/formulation.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low.** High-volume drug, standard MedDRA PTs, a signal so large it
is trivially detectable. The work is entirely interpretive — distinguishing *this* giant number from
the giant numbers the site usually debunks.

## 2. The blind spot FAERS has for *how* a drug was used: an off-label-area warning you cannot see in the indication field

**Working title:** "Used Off-Label, Invisible On-Label: Why FAERS Can't Tell You Where the Kybella
Went — and What the New Warning Is Really About"

**Safety question.** On September 15, 2026 the FDA added labeling warning of serious adverse reactions
from using deoxycholic acid (Kybella) **outside** its one approved area (submental/under-chin fat) —
blurred vision, involuntary eye contractions, muscle and nerve damage, numbness, difficulty walking —
plus persistent nodules/masses. FDA's review counted **129** adverse-event cases tied to use outside
the chin and **117** unresolved nodule/mass cases. The methodological hook is unusually clean: FAERS
records the *drug* and the *event*, but not the *body region injected* or whether use was on- or
off-label. So a signal driven almost entirely by off-label administration is **structurally
confounded by an unobserved exposure variable** — the article can show what disproportionality *can*
surface (a neuro/vascular-injury cluster disproportionate for a cosmetic injectable) and what it
*cannot* (that the harm is concentrated in off-label use), and why that gap matters for reading any
signal on a drug with heavy off-label use.

**Why it matters now.** The FDA labeling action is dated **September 15, 2026**; FDA says it approved
the revised labeling after reviewing FAERS (now the AEMS database) reports and the medical literature,
and that the nodule/mass reactions occur with both approved under-chin use and off-label use. This is
a this-month regulatory action with a spontaneous-reporting spine, and — like Idea #1 — it explicitly
names the AEMS-era data this site is built around.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) Four-method engine for deoxycholic acid against
the neuro/ophthalmic/vascular-injury PT cluster (*Vision blurred*, *Eyelid myokymia*/*Eye movement
disorder*, *Nerve injury*, *Facial paralysis*, *Peripheral motor neuropathy*, *Gait disturbance*,
*Skin necrosis*, *Injection site necrosis*) and the persistent-mass cluster (*Nodule*, *Mass*,
*Injection site mass*), with observed counts beside every EB05 and the quarterly caterpillar. (2) The
teaching payload — the **unobserved-exposure / use-pattern blind spot**: FAERS has no "anatomic site"
or "on-label vs off-label" field, so even a clear event signal cannot be attributed to the off-label
practice the warning targets; this is a transferable lesson for *any* drug with substantial off-label
use (and a companion to the 07-24 sevoflurane "invisible subpopulation" idea — same family of "the
answer isn't in the table," but here the hidden variable is *route/site of administration*, not
genotype/ancestry). (3) Honest caveats: narrow indication → modest volume; cosmetic/medspa reporting
channels differ from standard clinical reporting.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** Moderate report volume; the event PTs are standard. The real
work is framing the negative result correctly — the point is precisely what the data *can't* resolve,
so the article must avoid over-reading a modest signal as confirmation of the off-label mechanism.

## 3. When the signal is exactly what the trial already told you — so the only new information is *when*

**Working title:** "The Label Already Says ARIA: What Time-to-Onset (Not Disproportionality) Adds for
Lecanemab and Donanemab"

**Safety question.** For the anti-amyloid Alzheimer's antibodies lecanemab (Leqembi) and donanemab
(Kisunla), ARIA — amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (edema/effusion, ARIA-E; microhaemorrhage,
ARIA-H) — is the defining, trial-characterized, label-warned toxicity. So running disproportionality
against ARIA is the pharmacovigilance equivalent of confirming water is wet: the expected effect
trivially dominates every method (as with boxed-warning cytotoxic-ADC toxicities). The honest question:
**once the signal is already known and labeled, what does FAERS actually add?** The recent literature
answers with two things disproportionality-for-detection does not: (a) **time-to-onset** structure
(serious ARIA clusters in the first ~1–2 months; Weibull "early-failure" shape), and (b) a
**within-class contrast** — lecanemab skews toward ARIA-H, donanemab toward ARIA-E. This is a chance to
teach that the *detection* question ("is there a signal?") is the wrong one for a known effect, and that
the useful FAERS questions are *timing* and *comparative profile*.

**Why it matters now.** Multiple 2026 FAERS analyses are fresh: an integrated FAERS + WHO-VigiAccess
study with report-level ARIA-E stratification was published **August 18, 2026** (serious outcomes
concentrated early, attenuating over time); a four-method (ROR/PRR/BCPNN/MGPS) lecanemab-vs-donanemab
comparison through 2025Q4 reported lecanemab stronger for ARIA-H and donanemab stronger for ARIA-E,
with median time-to-onset **46 days (lecanemab) vs 31 days (donanemab)** by Weibull analysis; a separate
donanemab study put median onset at **31.5 days**. With both antibodies now in real-world use, a careful
"how to read FAERS for a known effect" piece is timely and fits the methodology lane.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) Confirm the obvious first (ARIA-E/ARIA-H
dominate all four methods for both drugs) — then pivot to what matters. (2) Introduce **time-to-onset /
Weibull shape** to the site's toolkit (a method the site has not yet used): plot onset distributions and
show the early-risk window the label flags. (3) **Active within-class comparator** (lecanemab vs
donanemab, both anti-Aβ in the same indication) to reproduce the ARIA-H-vs-ARIA-E asymmetry — the
correct frame when a whole-database background is meaningless because the effect is universal in the
exposed. (4) Caveats: notoriety/launch-era reporting, channelling (which patients get which antibody),
small-to-moderate N, and the firm non-causal framing the site always keeps.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** Moderate report counts (thousands per drug), standard PTs.
Time-to-onset requires onset-date fields (incomplete in FAERS) — handling that missingness honestly is
part of the methods content.

  ARIA-E stratification for lecanemab and donanemab: an integrated FAERS and WHO-VigiAccess
  pharmacovigilance study," *Ther Adv Drug Saf* 2026 (publ. Aug 18, 2026) —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/20420986261473013)
- According to PubMed: Feng X, Bi S, Shi C, et al., "Comparison of safety of lecanemab and donanemab:
  a real-world disproportionality analysis using the FDA adverse event reporting system,"
  *Front Pharmacol* 2026;17:1868789 (ARIA-H vs ARIA-E asymmetry; TTO 46 vs 31 days) —

## 4. A brand-new non-opioid painkiller — where the right comparator is another painkiller, not the whole database

**Working title:** "You Can't Background a Pain Drug Against Everything: Reading Suzetrigine
(Journavx) With an Active-Analgesic Comparator"

**Safety question.** Suzetrigine (Journavx; VX-548), the first selective NaV1.8 inhibitor, was approved
January 30, 2025 as a non-opioid option for moderate-to-severe acute pain — a high-profile launch in the
opioid-crisis context. For a symptomatic pain drug, a whole-database disproportionality background is
badly contaminated: the comparator population is "everyone in FAERS," but pain patients differ
systematically (co-analgesics, the underlying painful condition, peri-operative context). A June 2026
FAERS study handled this by using a **comparator-referenced empirical-Bayes** design — suzetrigine
against acetaminophen, ibuprofen, and background "other drugs" — and isolated **suzetrigine-unique**
sensory signals (paraesthesia, burning sensation, hypoaesthesia) not fully characterized pre-approval.
Can the site reproduce the *idea* that for a new analgesic the honest reference is an **active
comparator in the same indication**, and show how the signal set changes versus a naive whole-database
run?

**Why it matters now.** The drug is new (approved **Jan 30, 2025**), so this is an early-post-marketing
"first fingerprint" setting (the pre-/early-exposure baseline theme, distinct from the queued teplizumab
and orforglipron ideas because the method, not the drug class, is the point). The anchoring analysis —
comparator-referenced EB with external triangulation against VigiBase — was published **June 11, 2026**,
analyzing the first ~8 months of reports and flagging neurological/sensory PTs (EB q05 11.4–31.2) as the
prominent, not-fully-labeled signals. Timely, topical (non-opioid analgesia), and squarely methodological.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) Show the failure mode: suzetrigine vs a
whole-database background produces a signal set shaped by the pain indication and co-reported analgesics.
(2) The fix: rebuild the reference set as an **active-comparator cohort** (acetaminophen, ibuprofen, and
— carefully — an opioid comparator) and recompute EB05/BCPNN for the sensory cluster (*Paraesthesia*,
*Burning sensation*, *Hypoaesthesia*, *Skin burning sensation*) vs the musculoskeletal/cutaneous cluster
(*Muscle spasms*, *Pruritus*, *Rash*), with observed counts and the quarterly caterpillar over the short
available window. (3) Lesson: comparator choice *is* the analysis for a symptomatic drug — and small-N,
early-launch signals need the trajectory before they mean anything (the site's standing caveat). Keep the
non-causal framing and the stimulated-reporting caveat (a heavily-publicized "first new painkiller in
decades" invites reporting).

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium–High.** New drug → short history and small counts; the
active-comparator contingency tables are simple but the honest handling of small N and the comparator
selection are the real work. Lower novelty risk than most because the method (comparator-referenced EB)
is the deliverable.

  NaV1.8 inhibitor suzetrigine: disproportionality analysis and flexible empirical bayes signals from
  FAERS with external triangulation," *Front Pharmacol* 2026;17:1814013 (publ. Jun 11, 2026) —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2026.1814013)
- IASP, "FDA Approves Non-Opioid Treatment for Moderate to Severe Acute Pain" (Journavx/suzetrigine

## VAERS watch (standing candidates — not fresh this week)

No Sept–Oct 2026 vaccine/VAERS regulatory action surfaced. Two real, dated-but-stale candidates remain
available if the batch needs VAERS balance; both would need a freshness note (as the 07-24 IXCHIQ idea did):

- **RSV-vaccine Guillain-Barré (Abrysvo/Arexvy).** A positive methodology contrast to the site's usual
  "disproportionality overstates" pieces: a rare passive-surveillance signal (CDC reported ~4.4 and ~1.8
  GBS reports per million doses for Abrysvo and Arexvy, *above* expected background) that was then
  confirmed by a controlled Medicare study (~9 and ~7 excess cases/million) and driven into both labels.
  The lesson: a rare-event VAERS signal earns belief only when an **observed-vs-expected** comparison
  (which raw disproportionality lacks) and a controlled study back it. Regulatory anchor is 2025, not fresh.
  Source: Practical Neurology, "Warning for Guillain-Barré Syndrome Added to the Labels of 2 RSV Vaccines"
  (https://practicalneurology.com/news/warning-for-guillain-barre-syndrome-added-to-the-labels-of-2-rsv-vaccines/2470942/);
