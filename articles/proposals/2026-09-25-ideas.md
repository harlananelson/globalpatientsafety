last ~1–2 months (August–September 2026), matched to what this site already does:
independent disproportionality reanalysis of FAERS/VAERS using the four-method engine
(GPS/EBGM, PRR, ROR, BCPNN/IC), EB05-thresholded signals with ≥2 of 4 methods agreeing,
latest-quarter snapshot **plus** a quarterly caterpillar trajectory, and observed counts
shown beside every EB05.

**Editorial lane (per `ARTICLE_QUEUE.md`):** methodology first. Harlan is a statistician,
not an MD, so each piece is framed as a transferable "reading FDA adverse-event data"
lesson anchored to a concrete drug/vaccine example — *not* a clinical causation claim.
Voice reference: the Christine Cotton VAERS piece — modest, testable claims; explicit about
what disproportionality can and cannot show; careful with stimulated-reporting, confounding

## 1. A boxed warning that took four escalations — reading the ferric carboxymaltose hypophosphatemia signal

**Working title:** "Escalated Four Times: What the New Injectafer Boxed Warning Teaches About
Reading a Label as a Natural Experiment (and Why the Loud Mechanism Hides the Silent Endpoint)"

**Safety question.** IV ferric carboxymaltose (Injectafer) causes symptomatic
hypophosphatemia, and the FDA has now escalated that risk to a **Boxed Warning** — its most
prominent category — after prior label revisions failed to bring reporting down. Two linked
questions the site is built to examine: (a) does FAERS disproportionality for ferric
carboxymaltose vs a hypophosphatemia/osteomalacia/fracture PT cluster corroborate the
regulator's decision, and (b) is this an **agent-specific** problem or an **IV-iron class**
problem — i.e., does ferric carboxymaltose flag harder than its within-class comparators
(ferumoxytol, iron sucrose, iron derisomaltose, sodium ferric gluconate)?

**Why it matters now.** On or about **September 1, 2026**, FDA issued a Drug Safety
Communication announcing a Boxed Warning added to the ferric carboxymaltose label for
symptomatic hypophosphatemia, citing **134 reports and published cases of serious,
symptomatic hypophosphatemia between July 1, 2022 and June 30, 2025** — some severe,
prolonged, or with serious outcomes — despite earlier warnings added in **February 2020**
and revised in **2023** (the 2023 revision already named osteomalacia and fractures as
long-term complications and recommended phosphate monitoring). This is a rare, fully dated
sequence of escalating regulatory action on a widely used product — a clean natural
experiment for the "does the label track the data?" question.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Run GPS/EBGM + PRR + ROR + BCPNN for
`drug = "ferric carboxymaltose"` against a PT cluster — *Hypophosphataemia*, *Blood
phosphorus decreased*, *Osteomalacia*, *Bone pain*, *Pathological fracture*, *Fatigue*,
*Muscular weakness*. Report the latest-quarter snapshot with observed counts and a quarterly
EBGM caterpillar. Two methodology centerpieces: (1) an **active-comparator class check** —
ferric carboxymaltose vs each other IV iron, since vs-background disproportionality for any
IV iron is inflated by the shared indication (iron-deficiency anemia); the *comparator*,
not the background, is where the agent-specific story lives. (2) The **upstream-mechanism
vs downstream-endpoint** lesson already in the queue's gotcha catalogue: hypophosphatemia
(a loud, frequently coded lab value) is the mechanism, while the outcomes that actually
matter — osteomalacia and fragility fractures — are rare, late, and under-reported in FAERS,
so a strong phosphate signal beside a near-silent fracture signal is *expected*, not
reassuring. Overlay the 2020 / 2023 / 2026 label dates on the caterpillar to test whether
reporting responded (stimulated-reporting caveat runs the other way here — a Boxed Warning
will itself lift future counts).

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low.** Ferric carboxymaltose is high-volume in FAERS and
*Hypophosphataemia* is a standard PT; the mechanism signal should be well-populated. The
harder, more interesting cut is the fracture/osteomalacia endpoint (small N — needs the
trajectory and honest observed counts) and clean generic-name normalization across the IV
iron comparators.

## 2. Two SJS/TEN label changes in one month — and why the co-reported drugs decide who to blame

**Working title:** "Blame the Bystander? SJS/TEN Warnings Land on Belimumab and Ryaltris the
Same Month — a Lesson in Confounding by Comedication"

**Safety question.** Stevens-Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN) is the
textbook trap for spontaneous-reporting data: it is rare, catastrophic, heavily co-reported,
and dominated by a handful of classic culprits (allopurinol, sulfonamides, aromatic
anticonvulsants, NSAIDs, certain antibiotics). When a new SJS/TEN warning lands on a drug
used in a **polypharmacy indication**, the honest question is: is the disproportionality
signal the drug, or the company it keeps? Belimumab (Benlysta) treats systemic lupus — a
population that routinely co-takes several top SJS/TEN culprits — which makes it a near-ideal
worked example of **innocent-bystander / confounding-by-comedication**. Ryaltris (a
nasal-spray combination) is the contrast: a very different reporting ecosystem for the
*same* endpoint.

**Why it matters now.** A **September 2026** FDA/label roundup reported new SJS/TEN warnings
added to both **Benlysta (belimumab)** — a widely used lupus and lupus-nephritis biologic —
and **Ryaltris**, describing the same class of serious skin reaction and instructing
immediate discontinuation on onset. Two SJS/TEN label changes surfacing the same month on
two mechanistically unrelated products is a timely hook for the methodology, not a causal
claim about either drug.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Run all four methods for
`drug = "belimumab"` and `drug = "olopatadine"`/`"mometasone"` (Ryaltris components) against
the SJS/TEN PT cluster — *Stevens-Johnson syndrome*, *Toxic epidermal necrolysis*,
*Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms*, *Erythema multiforme*,
*Skin exfoliation*. Show observed counts and the caterpillar. The methodology centerpiece is
a **co-reported-drug decomposition**: within belimumab SJS/TEN reports, tabulate the
concomitant medications and show how much of the raw signal rides along with known culprits
(allopurinol, lamotrigine, sulfamethoxazole, NSAIDs) that lupus patients commonly take — the
disproportionality engine cannot separate the suspect from its neighbors, so a positive EB05
is a *starting* question, not an answer. Pair with the small-N fragility caveat (SJS/TEN
counts are tiny; a single litigation or publicity cluster can move an ROR by an order of
magnitude) and contrast the two drugs' co-reporting profiles to show why the same endpoint
warrants different skepticism depending on the surrounding reports. *Distinct from* the
queued "permanent-deafness ROR-10,000 small-N mirage" idea: that piece is about a huge number
being an artifact; this one is about **attributing** a real-looking signal to the wrong drug
in a polypharmacy population.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** Belimumab is well-reported, but SJS/TEN PTs are
rare, so the drug-event cells are small — the trajectory and observed counts must carry the
narrative, and the concomitant-medication decomposition needs careful handling of the
FAERS drug role (primary vs secondary vs concomitant suspect). Ryaltris is a newer, lower-
volume combination product, so its arm may be too sparse to do more than a snapshot — worth
confirming before committing to a two-arm structure.

**Citations.**
- askapatient Drug Safety Update, September 2026 (new SJS/TEN warnings for Benlysta and
  Ryaltris) — https://news.askapatient.com/p/drug-safety-update-september-2026-ryaltris-beers-list-update-atherosclerosis-study
- DailyMed, BENLYSTA (belimumab) current label —
  https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/search.cfm?labeltype=human&query=Belimumab
- FDA Drug Safety Communications index (for the primary label-change record) —
  https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/drug-safety-communications

## 3. When a disproportionality table is *right and wrong at the same time* — the retroperitoneal-fibrosis case

**Working title:** "The Ergots Are Real; the Beta-Blockers Are Bystanders — Sorting One
FAERS Results Table Into Signal and Confounding"

**Safety question.** A single disproportionality table almost always mixes genuine
drug-induced signals with artifacts of the shared patient population — and the statistics
alone cannot tell you which is which. A just-published FAERS analysis of drug-associated
retroperitoneal fibrosis (RPF) is a near-perfect teaching table: it surfaces the
**mechanistically established** ergot-derived culprits (methysergide, pergolide,
bromocriptine — RPF from these is textbook) *alongside* widely used cardiovascular and
metabolic drugs (bisoprolol, atenolol, metformin, propranolol) whose "signals" are far more
plausibly **confounding by indication / shared population / protopathic** effects. Can our
four-method engine, under the EB05 ≥ 2 + ≥2/4 rule, reproduce the ergot signals — and does
applying external pharmacology (not more statistics) correctly demote the bystanders?

**Why it matters now.** Baldini et al., *Pharmacological Reports*, published **September 11,
2026**, queried FAERS (1969–2025; 30,668,520 total reports), found 1,013 RPF cases, and
reported the strongest associations for **methysergide (ROR 5332.28), pergolide (ROR 923.85),
and bromocriptine (ROR 311.30)** — but also statistically significant disproportionality for
bisoprolol, atenolol, metformin, propranolol, etanercept, prednisone, rituximab, and
cyclophosphamide. It is a fresh, dated, fully citable worked example of a table that is
simultaneously correct (ergots) and misleading (cardiometabolic bystanders).

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Reproduce the RPF PT signal
(*Retroperitoneal fibrosis*) for the ergot agents in our four-method engine, show the
latest-quarter snapshot + caterpillar + observed counts, and confirm the ergots clear our
threshold comfortably. Then the methodology centerpiece: take the cardiovascular/metabolic
"signals" and show *why* disproportionality flags them without causation — patients on
beta-blockers and metformin are older, poly-medicated, and heavily imaged (RPF is often an
incidental CT finding), so co-occurrence with an RPF report is a population artifact, and
protopathic bias (early RPF symptoms → antihypertensive prescribing) can run the arrow
backwards. Transferable lesson for the queue's gotcha catalogue: **the raw leaderboard lies —
a results table is a hypothesis generator, and separating real from confounded requires
external mechanism knowledge, not a bigger EB05.** Optionally add a channeling/notoriety
check on the caterpillar for the ergots (their RPF reporting long predates FAERS and should
look flat/historical).

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** *Retroperitoneal fibrosis* is a standard PT
and the ergots are historically well-reported (though methysergide is largely withdrawn, so
its reports are old — pool quarters or note the era). The cardiometabolic arms are easy to
pull; the work is interpretive, not extractive.

**Citations.**
- Baldini S, et al. "Drugs associated with retroperitoneal fibrosis: assessment of the
  1969–2025 Food and Drug Administration (FDA) pharmacovigilance database." *Pharmacol Rep*,
  2026 Sep 11. (Retrieved via PubMed; PMID 42726440.)
  DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s43440-026-00894-y
- Background on ergot-derived drugs and retroperitoneal fibrosis is long established in the
  clinical literature; the article should cite a standard reference alongside the FAERS paper
  to make the "real signal" half of the table stand on mechanism, not disproportionality.

## 4. The comparator makes the signal — a three-arm gabapentinoid ocular study as a worked example

**Working title:** "Compared to *What*? How the Choice of Comparator Turns a Gabapentinoid
Eye-Disorder Signal On and Off"

**Safety question.** Every disproportionality number is implicitly a comparison — usually
against the whole database. But "vs everything" bakes in the entire background case-mix, and
switching to an **active comparator** (a drug used for the same indication) can make a signal
appear, shrink, or vanish. A recent three-arm study of gabapentinoids (gabapentin,
pregabalin) and ocular disorders makes this explicit — it triangulates FAERS
disproportionality against **active comparators** (diazepam, amitriptyline, carbamazepine)
plus social-listening (Reddit) and a literature review. It is an ideal vehicle to teach the
comparator-choice lesson and the promise-and-peril of multi-source triangulation.

**Why it matters now.** Murray et al., *Pharmaceuticals (Basel)*, published **July 27, 2026**,
reported positive FAERS signals for eye disorders with both gabapentinoids against active
controls, with **pregabalin showing higher reporting odds than gabapentin for cataract
(ROR 4.55; 95% CI 2.51–8.26) and blindness (ROR 2.63; 95% CI 1.85–3.73)**, flagged a
discrepancy between FAERS and the manufacturer labeling, and noted a plausible retinal
mechanism (CACNA2D1). Gabapentinoids are among the most-prescribed drugs in the US, so the
public-health surface is large and the FAERS data are abundant — a good, current, citable
anchor for a purely methodological piece.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Run all four methods for
`drug ∈ {gabapentin, pregabalin}` against an ocular PT cluster (*Cataract*, *Blindness*,
*Vision blurred*, *Diplopia*, *Photophobia*, *Retinal disorder*) under **three explicit
denominators**: (a) vs the full database, (b) vs the paper's active comparators, and (c) a
head-to-head pregabalin-vs-gabapentin cut — and show side by side how EB05/ROR move as the
comparator changes, with observed counts and caterpillars throughout. The methodology
centerpiece is the **denominator lesson**: a signal is only as meaningful as the comparison
it is drawn against, and reporting the comparator is as important as reporting the number.
Secondary thread: the **triangulation caveat** — social-listening (Reddit) and spontaneous
reports share the same stimulated-reporting and notoriety biases, so agreement across them is
weaker corroboration than it looks; convergence is reassuring only when the sources have
*independent* failure modes.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low.** Both drugs are very high-volume in FAERS; ocular PTs
are standard. The only care needed is consistent construction of the active-comparator
denominators so the three arms are truly comparable.

**Citations.**
- Murray M, et al. "Possible Signals of Ocular Disorders Associated with Gabapentinoids: A
  Three-Arm Study." *Pharmaceuticals (Basel)*, 2026 Jul 27. (Retrieved via PubMed;
  PMID 42653672.) DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/ph19081175

## 5. "It's not in the label" is not evidence — the drug-associated bipolar-disorder table

**Working title:** "Absence of a Label Warning Is Not Evidence of a Signal: Reading a
FAERS Bipolar-Disorder Table Without the Fallacy"

**Safety question.** A common rhetorical move in disproportionality papers is: "N of the
top-signal drugs carry no mention of this risk in their label — therefore the label is
missing something." But a drug can lack a label warning for many reasons (the association is
confounded, the endpoint is part of the treated disease, regulators triaged it as
non-actionable), so **"not in the label" is a weak argument, not a discovery.** Psychiatric
endpoints make this especially treacherous: the drug is often prescribed *to* a population
already at high risk of the outcome (confounding by indication / protopathic bias). Can we
take a fresh FAERS bipolar-disorder table, reproduce its top signals, and demonstrate how
much of the "unlabeled risk" narrative dissolves under indication and time-to-onset scrutiny?

**Why it matters now.** Du et al., *Psychiatry Research*, published **August 11, 2026**,
mined FAERS (2004 Q1–2025 Q3; 23,607,454 reports; 17,620 bipolar-disorder cases) and reported
that **44 of the top 50 drugs (88%) carried no mention of bipolar-disorder risk in their
prescribing information**, with the strongest signals for pizotifen, tafenoquine,
flupenthixol, naltrexone, and pemoline, varenicline contributing the most reports (537), and
**51% of cases arising within 0–30 days** of exposure. It is a current, dated, citable table
that leans on exactly the "missing from the label" framing this piece would interrogate.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** Reproduce the top bipolar-disorder signals
in our four-method engine (EB05 ≥ 2 + ≥2/4), with observed counts and caterpillars. Then two
methodology centerpieces: (1) the **label-gap fallacy** — walk through why several top drugs
plausibly lack a warning (e.g., naltrexone and varenicline are given to populations with high
baseline psychiatric comorbidity; a flupenthixol "signal" for bipolar disorder is
confounding by indication almost by construction, since it *is* a psychotropic). (2) A
**time-to-onset** dimension the site has not yet used: the paper's finding that half of cases
appear within 30 days is compatible with reverse causation and reporting artifact as much as
with a drug effect — so early onset is not corroboration. Transferable lesson: for
psychiatric (and other indication-linked) endpoints, disproportionality plus "absent from the
label" is close to no evidence at all; you need the confounding-by-indication check and the
onset distribution before the table means anything.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** The high-volume drugs (varenicline, naltrexone)
are easy; several top-signal agents (pizotifen, pemoline, flupenthixol) are old, niche, or
not US-marketed, so their arms will be sparse and era-bound — pool quarters and show observed
counts. Time-to-onset requires event-date fields that are inconsistently populated in FAERS,
so that thread should be framed as illustrative, not definitive.

**Citations.**
- Du P, et al. "Drug-associated bipolar disorder: A disproportionality analysis of the FAERS
  database." *Psychiatry Res*, 2026 Aug 11; 365:117375. (Retrieved via PubMed; PMID 42603523.)
  DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117375
