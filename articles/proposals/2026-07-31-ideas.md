## 1. The interaction is the signal: detecting a drug *pair* effect that neither drug shows alone

**Working title:** "Two Drugs, One Signal: How to Read a Drug–Drug-Interaction Signal in FAERS —
and Why Co-Reporting Isn't Interaction"

**Safety question.** Every article the site has published detects signals for *one* suspect drug.
But some of the most clinically important adverse effects are **interactions** — harms that appear
only when two drugs are taken together and are absent (or weak) for either alone. A just-published
FAERS study on **lithium + haloperidol** reports neurotoxicity signals (neuroleptic malignant
syndrome, encephalopathy, delirium, "neurotoxicity") that are more frequently reported for the
*combination* than for either agent without the other. The transferable question: how do you
actually detect an *interaction* signal in a spontaneous-reporting database, and — the gotcha —
why does a raw two-drug co-report **not** prove interaction (patients on both are sicker,
co-prescribed for refractory illness, and more heavily monitored)?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Zhou C, Zheng L, Wu X, Xiong J, Zhang S,
"Characterization and clinical prioritization of neurotoxicity-related safety signals associated
with lithium and haloperidol co-administration: A pharmacovigilance study based on FAERS,"
*J Affect Disord*, published **July 10, 2026** (FAERS Q1 2004–Q4 2025), used **report-level
dual-comparator screening** to retain signals specific to co-administration over either drug
alone, and prioritized neuroleptic malignant syndrome, neurotoxicity, encephalopathy, and
delirium ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2026.122241)). A second, near-simultaneous FAERS
paper applies the same *class* of interaction methods (Ω shrinkage, additive/multiplicative
models) to immune-checkpoint-inhibitor + glucocorticoid co-therapy
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/00185787261463742), *Hosp Pharm*, July 26, 2026) — so DDI signal
detection is a live, this-month methods theme, not a one-off. The lithium/haloperidol pairing is
also *mechanistically clean* (a classic, decades-old neurotoxicity concern) and both drugs are
high-volume — an ideal teaching case where the biology is uncontroversial and the **method** is
the lesson.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) Run the standard four-method engine three
ways — lithium-without-haloperidol, haloperidol-without-lithium, and the co-administration set —
against the neurotoxicity PT cluster (*Neuroleptic malignant syndrome*, *Encephalopathy*,
*Delirium*, *Serotonin syndrome*, *Tremor*, *Neurotoxicity*). (2) Introduce the interaction layer:
show why "both drugs on the same report" is the wrong comparator, and demonstrate a defensible
interaction contrast (the pair vs. each single agent, i.e. the dual-comparator idea) with observed
counts beside every estimate. (3) The methods payload — a genuinely **new gotcha for the
catalogue**: *co-medication confounding.* An interaction "signal" can be manufactured entirely by
channelling (the sickest patients get both drugs) and by surveillance (combination therapy is
watched harder). State plainly what a two-drug FAERS signal can (flag pairs for review) and cannot
(prove synergistic causation) establish. Caterpillar the combination signal to show stability vs.
attention-driven drift.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** Both drugs are high-volume with deep coverage and
standard PTs, so the three base analyses are easy. The real work is building the co-administration
subset cleanly (both listed as suspect/concomitant on one report) and choosing an honest
interaction comparator — which *is* the article's methods content, not overhead.

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Zhou C, Zheng L, Wu X, Xiong J, Zhang S, "Characterization and clinical
  prioritization of neurotoxicity-related safety signals associated with lithium and haloperidol
  co-administration: A pharmacovigilance study based on FAERS," *J Affect Disord* 2026;413:122241 —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2026.122241)
- According to PubMed: Zhang Z, Gao T, Li L, Tan X, Zeng P, "Drug-Drug Interactions Between Immune
  Checkpoint Inhibitors and Glucocorticoids: A Real-World Pharmacovigilance Analysis," *Hosp Pharm*

  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/00185787261463742)

## 2. When crude disproportionality isn't enough: adjusting for confounders on a rare cardiac signal

**Working title:** "Adjust, Then Believe: A Multivariable Look at the Teclistamab Myocarditis
Signal — and What Covariate Adjustment Can (and Can't) Fix"

**Safety question.** The site's four-method engine computes **crude** disproportionality — no
adjustment for age, sex, comorbidity, or concomitant drugs. A new FAERS study of bispecific
T-cell-engaging antibodies reports cardiovascular signals using **multivariable-adjusted reporting
odds ratios** instead, and finds agent-specific results: teclistamab carries the strongest
myocarditis signal (adjusted ROR ≈ 5.95 on just n = 4), disseminated intravascular coagulation is
sharply enriched for the class, and fatal cardiovascular events are elevated overall. Two live
teaching questions: (a) does adjustment change the picture versus a crude four-method read, and (b)
is an adjusted ROR of ~6 built on **four reports** something to trust — or the same small-N mirage
the site keeps flagging, now wearing a regression's clothing?

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Munir M, Sayed A, Lee DH, et al., "Cardiovascular
Toxicity Associated With Bispecific Antibodies in Hematological Malignancies: A Comprehensive
Pharmacovigilance Analysis," *Am J Hematol*, published **July 24, 2026** (FAERS Dec 2014–Sep 2025),
analyzed 7,647 bispecific-antibody reports with **multivariable logistic regression adjusting for
age, sex, cardiovascular comorbidity, disease, and concomitant cardiotoxic medications**, reporting
DIC (aROR 4.35), teclistamab myocarditis (aROR 5.95, n = 4), and elevated fatal CVAEs (aROR 1.63,
n = 447) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajh.70449)). Bispecifics (teclistamab, talquetamab,
elranatamab, epcoritamab, glofitamab, mosunetuzumab) are the fastest-expanding class in
hematologic oncology, so a careful "how to read an *adjusted* disproportionality result" piece is
both timely and methodologically fresh — the site has never shown covariate adjustment.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) Reproduce the crude four-method signal for
the bispecific class (and per agent) against a cardiac PT cluster — *Myocarditis*, *Cardiac
failure*, *Hypotension*, *Atrial fibrillation*, *Disseminated intravascular coagulation*, *Shock* —
with observed counts beside every EB05. (2) The methods centerpiece: contrast crude vs. adjusted.
Explain *why* one might adjust (these patients are older, sicker, on other cardiotoxic drugs, and
CRS/infection can drive hemodynamic events), demonstrate a simple stratified or logistic-adjusted
ROR on one well-populated endpoint (e.g. hypotension or DIC), and show that adjustment shifts a
well-populated signal modestly — **but cannot rescue an n = 4 myocarditis estimate**, whose CI is
enormous regardless of how many covariates you add. The transferable lesson: *adjustment fixes
confounding, not sparsity;* a regression on four events is still four events. Ties directly to the
site's "small-N EB05 needs the trajectory before it means anything" gotcha and extends it to
adjusted estimates.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium–High.** The bispecific agents are moderate-to-low volume
(recent launches), so several cardiac PTs are small-N — which is the point for the adjustment-
can't-fix-sparsity argument, but demands care. Building even a lightweight adjusted model on FAERS
(covariate extraction, CRS/infection flags) is more work than the site's usual crude pipeline; the
honest framing of adjustment's limits is the deliverable.

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Munir M, Sayed A, Lee DH, Ghazi S, Houmsse MM, Badat S, Moeed A, Guha A,
  Addison D, Epperla N, "Cardiovascular Toxicity Associated With Bispecific Antibodies in
  Hematological Malignancies: A Comprehensive Pharmacovigilance Analysis," *Am J Hematol* 2026 —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajh.70449)
- FDA, Tecvayli (teclistamab) prescribing information / boxed warning (CRS, neurologic toxicity)
  for label-anchor context — https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/tecvayli

## 3. Same class, different ways to die: differentiating *fatal* toxicity within the PARP inhibitors

**Working title:** "Not Whether, But How Often It Kills: Reading Case-Fatality Differences Across
the PARP Inhibitors in FAERS"

**Safety question.** The site's pieces mostly ask *whether* a signal exists. A new FAERS study asks
a harder, more useful question of a whole drug class: given that all four PARP inhibitors (olaparib,
niraparib, rucaparib, talazoparib) share hematologic toxicity, **do they differ in how lethal their
adverse events are** — and are those differences real or artifacts of who takes which drug? The
paper finds olaparib has *rare-but-highly-enriched* fatal events (interstitial lung disease,
pneumonitis, thrombosis) while niraparib has *more-frequent-but-moderately-increased* risks — a
crisp worked example of a distinction the site's catalogue hasn't drawn: **the shape of a toxicity
signature (rare-severe vs. common-moderate) matters as much as its height.**

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, Meng D, Yang Y, "Drug-specific differences in fatal
adverse events among PARP inhibitors: a FAERS pharmacovigilance study," *Clin Transl Oncol*,
published **July 28, 2026** (25,413 reports; 5,930 fatal), computed fatal-report proportions and
reporting odds ratios across the four agents, finding olaparib with higher fatal reporting odds
(OR 1.29 vs. talazoparib) and drug-specific fatal signatures — olaparib enriched for rare ILD/
pneumonitis/thrombosis, niraparib for more frequent moderate events, with fatal events occurring
*later* than non-fatal ones (olaparib median 84 vs. 48 days)
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12094-026-04511-5)). PARP inhibitors are widely used across
ovarian, breast, prostate, and pancreatic cancers, and the "which one is safest" question is
genuinely clinical — making the *methods* lesson (how to read case-fatality and time-to-onset, and
why channelling by cancer type confounds it) broadly relevant.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) Reproduce the class hematologic signal
(*Anaemia*, *Thrombocytopenia*, *Neutropenia*, *Myelodysplastic syndrome*) with the four-method
engine to establish the shared backbone. (2) The new methods content: compute the **fatal-report
fraction** and a fatal-vs-nonfatal reporting-odds contrast per agent for the divergent PTs (ILD/
pneumonitis, thrombosis), show observed counts, and lay the rare-severe (olaparib) profile beside
the common-moderate (niraparib) one. (3) The essential caveats, stated plainly: fatal-report
fraction is **not** case-fatality rate (reporting is selective toward serious/fatal outcomes);
**channelling by cancer type** is the dominant confounder (olaparib and niraparib are used in
different tumors with different baseline mortality — prostate/pancreatic reports skew fatal); and
time-to-onset from spontaneous reports is a reporting artifact as much as a biological one. Adds
two catalogue entries — *case-fatality ≠ fatal-report fraction* and *toxicity-signature shape* —
and reuses the natural-experiment/timing discipline the NAION piece established.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** All four drugs are moderate-volume with good coverage;
hematologic PTs are well-populated. The divergent fatal PTs (ILD, thrombosis) are smaller-N, and
disentangling channelling by tumor type from true drug differences is the analytically interesting
(and honest) part — likely unresolved, and worth saying so.

**Citations.**
- According to PubMed: Meng D, Yang Y, "Drug-specific differences in fatal adverse events among
  PARP inhibitors: a FAERS pharmacovigilance study," *Clin Transl Oncol* 2026 —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12094-026-04511-5)
- FDA, olaparib (Lynparza) label — MDS/AML and pneumonitis warnings, for regulatory anchor —
  https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm?event=overview.process&ApplNo=208558

## 4. A label being *loosened*: does the passive safety record agree with FDA walking back testosterone's warnings?

**Working title:** "The Warning FDA Is Removing: What FAERS Says About Testosterone,
Cardiovascular Events, and Prostate Cancer as the Label Relaxes"

**Safety question.** Almost every regulatory hook the site examines is a warning being *added*.
This one runs the other way. In June 2026 FDA requested that testosterone-therapy labels **remove**
the age-related-hypogonadism limitation of use and **soften** the cardiovascular and prostate-cancer
warnings, citing the TRAVERSE trial's null cardiovascular result. That inverts the usual
disproportionality exercise: instead of "does FAERS corroborate a new warning," the question becomes
"as FDA *retires* a longstanding warning, does the spontaneous-reporting record show a
cardiovascular/prostate signal that ever justified it — or one that persists despite the trial?"
It's a clean setting to teach that **passive disproportionality and randomized trials answer
different questions**, and that a FAERS cardiovascular "signal" for testosterone is exactly the kind
of confounded, indication-channeled, protopathic artifact the site warns about.

**Why it matters now.** On **June 18, 2026**, HHS/FDA announced requested class-wide labeling
updates for testosterone products — removing the limitation stating safety/effectiveness in
age-related hypogonadism was not established, narrowing the prostate-cancer contraindication to
metastatic disease, and revising BPH warnings — grounded in the **TRAVERSE** cardiovascular
outcomes trial (>5,200 men; MACE 7.0% testosterone vs. 7.3% placebo). This directly reverses the
direction of the 2015 class-wide cardiovascular warning, a rare and well-dated **natural experiment**
in the label itself. Testosterone is extremely high-volume in FAERS, so the analysis is
data-rich and the timing is squarely this-month.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **FAERS.** (1) Four-method engine for testosterone against a
cardiovascular PT cluster (*Myocardial infarction*, *Cerebrovascular accident*, *Deep vein
thrombosis*, *Pulmonary embolism*, *Cardiac failure*) and a prostate cluster (*Prostate cancer*,
*Prostatomegaly*, *Prostatism*), with observed counts and the quarterly caterpillar spanning the
2015 warning and the 2026 relaxation — a two-sided natural-experiment read. (2) The methods payload:
why a testosterone→prostate-cancer FAERS signal is a **detection/protopathic** artifact
(hypogonadal men on therapy are monitored more, so cancers are *found* more) and why a
testosterone→MI signal is **confounded by indication** (men prescribed testosterone are
cardiometabolically higher-risk at baseline) — then contrast both against TRAVERSE's null. The
transferable lesson: *disproportionality cannot adjudicate a causal question a randomized trial was
built to answer;* a stubborn FAERS signal alongside a null RCT is a lesson in what spontaneous
reports measure (reporting, channelling, surveillance), not incidence. Reuses the site's
protopathic-bias and confounding-by-indication threads in a novel "label going the other way" frame.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Low–Medium.** Testosterone is high-volume with deep coverage and
standard PTs; the base analysis is trivial. The value is conceptual (trial-vs-passive-data and the
walk-back framing) and in cleanly spanning the 2015→2026 label window on the caterpillar. Care is
needed to separate the well-documented reporting surge around the 2014–2015 FDA review and
litigation from any underlying pattern.

## 5. A live pediatric vaccine flagged for *failing* — reading the PROQUAD signals in VAERS honestly

**Working title:** "When 'Vaccination Failure' Is the Signal: Reading the Live MMRV Vaccine
(PROQUAD) in VAERS, With Subgroups Done Carefully"

**Safety question.** Two recent VAERS disproportionality studies of the live-attenuated MMRV
vaccine (measles-mumps-rubella-varicella; PROQUAD) surface an unusual mix: alongside expected
reactogenicity, they report **febrile convulsion**, **cyanosis**, influenza-like illness, and —
strikingly — **"vaccination failure"** as statistically significant signals not in the label, plus
a sharply elevated seizure signal *in children with pre-existing epilepsy* (ROR ≈ 10.3). This is a
rich methods case with two distinct lessons: (a) a **lack-of-efficacy PT** ("vaccination failure")
scoring as a disproportionality signal is a category error worth explaining — it is not an adverse
*reaction*; and (b) a subgroup signal (seizures in epileptic children) can be real, confounded by
susceptibility, or both — and the honest reading needs the baseline.

**Why it matters now.** According to PubMed, two pediatric VAERS analyses published within the last
two months: Liang G, Liu Q, Song Y, "Adverse events following measles, mumps, rubella and varicella
virus vaccine live (PROQUAD) reported to VAERS, 2015-2025," *Virol J*, **December 29, 2025**
(17,234 reports; unlabeled signals for febrile convulsion, vaccination failure, cyanosis, ILI;
serious/fatal outcomes concentrated in children under three)
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-025-03022-z)); and Yan K, Yang G, Yan L, et al., "MMRV
vaccine safety: A comprehensive analysis of adverse events recorded among pediatric populations,"
*Hum Vaccin Immunother*, **December 18, 2025** (13,724 reports; seizure risk in epileptic children
ROR 10.27, wheezing in asthmatic children ~6.8×, early-onset Weibull profile)
([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/21645515.2025.2590875)). Live-attenuated pediatric vaccine safety is
a persistently high-salience public topic, and these give a fresh, well-sourced VAERS spine.

**How the site's data/methods apply.** **VAERS.** (1) Four-method engine for PROQUAD/MMRV against
the reported clusters — reactogenicity (*Injection site erythema*, *Pyrexia*), neurologic
(*Febrile convulsion*, *Seizure*, *Cyanosis*), and the lack-of-efficacy PT (*Vaccination failure*,
*Drug ineffective*) — with observed counts and the caterpillar. (2) Methods payload #1 — a **new
catalogue entry**: *lack-of-efficacy PTs are not adverse reactions.* "Vaccination failure" scoring
high is a reporting/definitional artifact (breakthrough disease reported *as* an AE), the vaccine
analogue of the site's "disease-as-signal" confounding — worth a clean explanation. (3) Methods
payload #2 — **subgroup signals need a susceptibility baseline**: the epilepsy→seizure and
asthma→wheezing signals could reflect that already-susceptible children convulse/wheeze after any
febrile stimulus, not a vaccine-specific hazard; show why an age/comorbidity-stratified read with
the standard stimulated-reporting caveat is essential, and state plainly that VAERS gives relative
reporting patterns, not incidence or causation. Complements (does not duplicate) the queued
Zostavax/Shingrix live-vaccine idea, which is about a live vaccine flagging for *its own target
disease* in adults.

**Expected data difficulty.** **Medium.** MMRV/PROQUAD is well-represented in VAERS with standard
PTs, so the base analysis and caterpillar are straightforward. The interesting work is the
subgroup stratification (epilepsy/asthma flags from VAERS history text is imperfect) and framing
the "vaccination failure" signal correctly — both are the article's methods content.

  (VAERS), 2015-2025," *Virol J* 2025;22(1):400 —
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-025-03022-z)
